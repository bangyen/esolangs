\begin{abstract}
Fix real roots $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed.  For every
nonzero real polynomial $F=\sum_jf_jx^j$ of degree $D$ divisible by
$\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$, the $k$-th largest nonleading coefficient
magnitude is at least $|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$, uniformly in the
degree.  The rows $k\ge2$ are sharp at $(2,3)$ and $(2,3,5)$, and not sharp
at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$, where the $k=2$ infimum is $65/12$ against $5$;
both sides come from the same certificate.  Summing the rows bounds the
logarithmic coefficient mass $\Lambda(F)=\sum_j\log^+|f_j|$ of a monic
multiple below by $\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$, sharp in order at the first $L$
primes but degenerate as the roots approach $2$; Jensen's formula supplies a term
of order $L^2$ there, and together the two give
$\inf\Lambda\asymp L^2+\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$ over monic multiples, at every
root set with $L\ge2$.  The key tool compares the $\ell^1$ tails of
normalized exponential sums with prescribed positive-integer zeros: deleting
the largest zero together with the largest node strictly increases the tail,
and iterating gives a product bound over the initial run of consecutive
zeros, with equality only when all the zeros are consecutive.  As a
by-product, the same-sign hypothesis under which Dubickas and Jankauskas
evaluate the consecutive case holds at all distinct positive nodes, making
their bound $H(P)\ge|P(1)|$ unconditional for distinct real roots greater
than one.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Polynomial multiples; coefficient mass; reduced
height; Chebyshev systems; exponential sums.

\section{Introduction}

For a nonzero $F(x)=\sum_jf_jx^j$ of degree $D$, define its logarithmic coefficient mass by
\[
  \Lambda(F)=\sum_{j:f_j\ne0}\log^+|f_j|,
  \qquad \log^+x=\max(0,\log x),
\]
where all logarithms are natural.  This is the quantity to bound because it
is additive over coefficients: a lower bound on each of several coefficient
order statistics adds one term to $\Lambda$, so the order-statistic bounds
proved below add up to a bound for $\Lambda$ that no bound on a single
height can give.  Throughout, $\N_{>0}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and
every asymptotic statement is for $L\to\infty$ with absolute implied
constants unless parameters are named.

Reduced length and reduced height minimize the two classical coefficient
norms over normalized multiples (monic ones for the reduced height; for the
reduced length, those with leading or constant coefficient
one)~\cite{dubickas,schinzel,schinzel-ii,schinzel-complex,dj}.  The Euclidean
analogue is effectively computable~\cite{frw}.  Dual
problems---an integer or bounded-height multiple, or a root of prescribed
multiplicity for a restricted-coefficient power series---form a classical
line~\cite{mignotte,bombieri-vaaler,bbbp-multiple,bbbp-ray}.  Work on sparse
multiples looks for multiples of prescribed degree, sparsity and
height~\cite{grt}.  The height
$\max_j|f_j|$ sees only one coefficient, and the length $\sum_j|f_j|$ can be
spread over many comparable terms, so neither norm forces many coefficients
to be large at once.  The mass $\Lambda$ does: it adds up the contributions
of every coefficient of magnitude greater than one, and equals the logarithm
of the multiplicative height,
$\Lambda(F)=\log\prod_{j:f_j\ne0}\max(1,|f_j|)$.  For integer coefficients,
$\Lambda(F)/\log B$ is roughly the total number of base-$B$ digits in the
nonzero coefficients, up to an additive term proportional to their number and
ignoring signs, zero entries and exponents.  For real coefficients it is
simply a mass.  In this sense the
results below are distributional: many coefficients must be large, not just
the largest.

Because $\log^+$ ignores coefficients of magnitude at most one, $\Lambda$ is
not scale invariant: $\Lambda(\varepsilon F)\to0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$, so no
lower bound for $\Lambda$ can hold without fixing a scale.  Every bound below
is therefore stated relative to the leading coefficient $f_D$, and the
normalization of interest is the monic one $|f_D|=1$, or more generally
$|f_D|\ge1$.  This is the normalization already used for the reduced
height~\cite{dj}, and for integer multiples it costs nothing.

It is worth saying what is gained over the standard route through the Mahler
measure $\mathcal M$.  A monic multiple $F$ of $\prod_i(x-r_i)$ has
$\mathcal M(F)\ge\prod_ir_i$, while Landau's inequality
$\mathcal M(F)\le\lVert F\rVert_2\le\sqrt{D+1}\,H(F)$ bounds the height from
below, so
\[
  \Lambda(F)\ \ge\ \log H(F)\ \ge\ \sum_{i=1}^L\log r_i-\tfrac12\log(D+1).
\]
For the first $L$ primes the sum is $\sim L\log L$, so this route gives order
$L\log L$: it charges only one coefficient, and the number of prescribed
roots enters once.  Corollary~\ref{cor:prime} gives
$\Lambda(F)\ge(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$, larger by a factor of order $L$ and
with no degree term at all, because each of the $L$ roots is charged against
a coefficient of its own.

Dubickas and Jankauskas associate to reciprocal roots and a prescribed zero
set $J$ a normalized determinant sequence $S_n(J)$ with $S_0(J)=1$ and
$S_k(J)=0$ for $k\in J$~\cite{dj}.  This sequence is the exponential sum $w$
studied below, and the reciprocal of its infinite tail is their dual lower
bound for the reduced height, their inequality~(8).  They evaluate the
consecutive case $J=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$ explicitly, on the condition that all
$S_n$ with $n$ large have the same sign: their $H(P)\ge|P(1)|$ holds
``provided that all $S_n$ \ldots have the same sign''~\cite[p.~333]{dj}.
That condition stays a hypothesis through their
Corollary~7~\cite[Section~2]{dj} and is checked only case by
case~\cite[Sections~3--4]{dj}.  Our consecutive base case,
Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, proves it for all distinct positive real nodes
by reading the sign off the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials $h_j$.
Their bound $H(P)\ge|P(1)|$ is then the $k=1$ row of
Theorem~\ref{thm:order}, made unconditional by Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}
for distinct real roots greater than one; the rows $k\ge2$ are new.

The main result bounds how the coefficient sizes of a multiple are
distributed.  For roots $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed,
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} (via the certificate of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack})
bounds all $L$ coefficient order statistics of every multiple, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} adds them up into a lower bound for $\Lambda$.
Writing $b_k(F)$ for the $k$-th largest of the nonleading
coefficient magnitudes $|f_j|$, $j<D$,
\[
  b_k(F)\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^{L}(r_i-1),
  \quad(1\le k\le L),
  \qquad
  \Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1).
\]
Both bounds are uniform in the degree and in any further roots of the
multiple.  The idea is simple to state.  To bound the $k$-th largest
coefficient magnitude, exclude any $k-1$ coefficient positions, and build a
normalized exponential sum in the reciprocal roots, the certificate, that
vanishes at those positions and at enough initial ones.  A bound on the
remaining tail of this sum, the sum of the magnitudes of its later terms,
then forces some coefficient outside the excluded set to be large.  All the
difficulty lies in that tail bound, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}: it
bounds the tail of a certificate by a product over its initial run of
consecutive zeros, with equality only when all the zeros are consecutive.

Inequality~(8) of~\cite{dj} already allows a prescribed set of positions to
be excluded from the certificate.  What is new here is a tail estimate for
arbitrary prescribed integer zero sets that depends on their initial run of
consecutive zeros.  If the largest prescribed zero is displaced, that is,
not part of that run, deleting it together with the largest reciprocal node
strictly increases the tail, even though moving a zero at fixed nodes can
push the tail either way.  Applying the estimate repeatedly over the
excluded positions turns one certificate into lower bounds for successive
coefficient order statistics, where earlier certificates controlled only a
single height.

Several consequences follow directly.  Corollary~\ref{cor:complex} extends the bound to complex multiples,
Corollary~\ref{cor:prime} specializes to prime roots,
Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum} sharpens the resulting asymptotic to the infimum
over monic real multiples, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:near} shows that for every root set here the root product
itself comes within $O(L^2)$ of the corresponding infimum over monic
multiples.

Section~\ref{sec:sharp} turns to sharpness.  It
shows that the $k=2$ bound is approached at roots $(2,3)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp23}) and the $k=2$ and $k=3$ bounds at
$(2,3,5)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp235}), so these bounds cannot be
improved by any constant factor in general.  They are not sharp at every
root set, however:
Proposition~\ref{prop:nonsharp} evaluates the $k=2$ infimum at roots
$(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$ as $65/12$, above the value $5$ that
\eqref{eq:order} gives, and obtains both the improved lower bound and the
matching family from the same certificate machinery.  Nor is the loss
bounded: Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded} exhibits distinct roots at which
a certificate bound for $b_1$ exceeds $|P(1)|$ by a factor at least of
order $L$, and gives the uniform bound
$b_1\ge|f_D|(r_1+\cdots+r_L)/2$, which neither implies nor is implied by the
product bound.  At $r_i=2$, where \eqref{eq:mass} is empty,
Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} still forces two large coefficients.
Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic}, proved by Jensen's formula rather than by a
certificate, adds the bound $\Lambda(F)\ge L^2/80$ for monic multiples when
$L\ge8$, at every root set with $r_i\ge2$, and Corollary~\ref{cor:allroots}
combines the two into $\inf_Q\Lambda(PQ)\asymp L^2+\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$,
uniformly in the root set once $L\ge2$.

\section{A displaced-zero tail theorem}

Let $\rho_1>\cdots>\rho_c\ge2$ and $y_i=\rho_i^{-1}$, so that
$0<y_1<y_2<\cdots<y_c\le1/2$.  For any $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ of size $c-1$, the
generalized Vandermonde system
\[
  w_d=\sum_{i=1}^c a_i y_i^d,
  \qquad w_0=1,
  \qquad w_z=0\quad(z\in Z),
\]
determines the coefficients $a_i$ uniquely.  Indeed, a kernel vector would be
a nonzero $c$-node exponential sum with the $c$ zeros $\{0\}\cup Z$,
contradicting the zero bound below.  It is convenient to regard the sequence
$w_0,w_1,\ldots$ as the restriction to $d\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ of the
real-analytic function $d\mapsto\sum_ia_iy_i^d$; all statements about zeros
and signs of an exponential sum refer to this function of the real variable.

\paragraph{Zero bound.}
A nonzero exponential sum on $r$ distinct positive nodes has at most $r-1$
real zeros, counted with multiplicity: write it as $\sum_ia_ie^{\lambda_id}$
with distinct $\lambda_i$, factor out one exponential, and induct on $r$ by
Rolle's theorem, the derivative being a sum on one fewer
node~\cite[Part~Five, Ch.~1]{polya-szego}; equivalently, the $e^{\lambda d}$
form a Chebyshev system~\cite{karlin}.
Consequently a nonzero $r$-node sum with $r-1$
distinct prescribed real zeros has exactly those zeros, each is simple, and the sum
changes sign at each of them.

\begin{lemma}[Consecutive zero set]\label{lem:consecutive}
Let $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c<1$; no cutoff $y_c\le1/2$ is imposed.  Let $w$ be the
exponential sum on these nodes with $w_0=1$ and $w_z=0$ for $z\in\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$;
it exists and is unique by the Vandermonde argument above, which needs only
distinct positive nodes.  Then
\[
  w_d=(-1)^{c+1}\Bigl(\prod_iy_i\Bigr)h_{d-c}(y_1,\ldots,y_c)
  \qquad(d\ge c),
\]
where $h_j$ is the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial, so these terms
are one-signed and
\[
  \Tail(w):=\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|=\prod_{i=1}^c\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put
\[
  U(x)=\sum_{d\ge0}w_dx^d=\frac{N(x)}{\prod_i(1-y_ix)},
  \qquad \deg N\le c-1.
\]
The first $c-1$ nonconstant coefficients vanish, so $N(x)-\prod_i(1-y_ix)$
is a constant multiple of $x^c$; that constant is fixed by the vanishing
$x^c$ coefficient of $N$ (which has degree at most $c-1$), giving
\[
  N(x)=\prod_i(1-y_ix)+(-1)^{c+1}\Bigl(\prod_i y_i\Bigr)x^c .
\]
Since $1/\prod_i(1-y_ix)=\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y_1,\ldots,y_c)x^j$, the displayed
formula for $w_d$ follows, and summing the one-signed terms gives
\[
  \Tail(w)=\Bigl(\prod_i y_i\Bigr)\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y_1,\ldots,y_c)
          =\prod_i\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Displaced-zero tail bound]\label{thm:tail}
Let $\rho_1>\cdots>\rho_c\ge2$, $y_i=\rho_i^{-1}$, $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ with
$|Z|=c-1$, and let $w$ be the sum determined above.  Put
$\ell=\max\{0\le k\le c-1:\{1,\ldots,k\}\subseteq Z\}$, the leading run of
$Z$; the elements of $Z$ outside $\{1,\ldots,\ell\}$ are its displaced zeros.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
  \Tail(w)=\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|
  \le \prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}
  =\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}(\rho_i-1)}.
\end{equation}
Equality holds exactly when $Z=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$.
\end{theorem}

For a concrete two-node example, take $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(3,2)$.  If the
prescribed zero is consecutive, $Z=\{1\}$, then
\[
 w_d=3^{1-d}-2^{1-d},\qquad \Tail(w)=\frac12,
\]
so equality holds in \eqref{eq:tail}.  Moving the zero to $Z=\{2\}$ gives
\[
 w_d=\frac95\,3^{-d}-\frac45\,2^{-d},\qquad \Tail(w)=\frac3{10}<\frac12.
\]
Here moving the zero lowers the tail, but that does not happen in general.
For $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(5,3)$, $Z=\{1\}$ gives $\Tail(w)=1/8$ while $Z=\{2\}$
gives $\Tail(w)=9/64>1/8$, so a displaced tail can exceed the consecutive
one.  The theorem therefore does not compare zero sets at fixed nodes.
Instead it removes the largest prescribed zero and the largest reciprocal
node together, which lowers the number of nodes.  Each displaced zero adds a
sign change relative to a comparison sum, and the proof shows that this sign
change lowers the total tail.  The proof handles the displaced zeros one at
a time, starting with the last.

The hard step compares a correction sum at the marked points of the zero set
with its value just past them.  Marked points come in two kinds: some force
the correction sum to vanish, and others only fix its sign.  We state this
comparison as a separate lemma.  Its proof is a zero count, in which the
sign-fixing points force extra zeros in each gap between them.

\begin{lemma}[Interlacing of the correction sum]\label{lem:interlace}
Let $c\ge2$ and $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c$, let $Z'\subset\N_{>0}$ satisfy $|Z'|=c-2$, and put
$m=\max(Z'\cup\{0\})$; the points of $\{0\}\cup Z'$ are the marked
points.  Let $\widehat F$ be the exponential sum on
$y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ vanishing on $Z'$, and $G$ the sum on
$y_1,\ldots,y_c$ vanishing on $\{0\}\cup Z'$.  Each is determined by those
conditions up to a scalar and, by the zero bound, has constant sign for
$d>m$; normalize both to be positive there.  Write $E_d=G_d-y_cG_{d-1}$,
$\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat F_{m+1}$, and
\[
  \Delta_d=\sigma\widehat F_d-E_d.
\]
Then $\Delta_d\ge0$ for every $d\ge m+1$.  No upper bound on the nodes is
needed.
\end{lemma}

Before the proof, here is an example of the counting.  Take
$c=5$ and $Z'=\{2,4,6\}$, so the marked set is $\{0\}\cup Z'=\{0,2,4,6\}$ and
$m=6$.  No two marked points are adjacent, so none of them forces a zero of
$\Delta$.  Instead $\Delta$ is nonzero at $2,4,6$, with signs $-1,+1,-1$.
So each of the gaps $(2,4)$ and $(4,6)$ contains an odd number of zeros of
the $4$-node sum $\Delta$, hence at least one.  Together with the zero at
$m+1=7$ this uses up the whole budget of $c-2=3$ zeros, so there are none
above $7$, and $\Delta$ is positive there.  In tabular form, writing
$\bullet$ for a marked point and listing the sign of $\Delta$ at it:
\[
  \begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
    d & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7\\\hline
    \text{marked} & \bullet & & \bullet & & \bullet & & \bullet & \\
    \sgn\Delta_d  &         & & -       & & +       & & -       & 0\\
    \text{forced zeros in gap} & & & & 0 & & 0 & &
  \end{array}
\]
The two sign changes, across $(2,4)$ and $(4,6)$, each cost a zero; with the
zero at $7$ that is three, the whole budget.  If instead $Z'=\{2,4,5,7\}$
($c=6$), the adjacent pair $\{4,5\}$ forces a zero of $\Delta$ at $5$, while
$\Delta$ is negative at both $4$ and $7$.  Since the signs at $4$ and $7$
agree, $(4,7)$ must contain an even number of zeros, so the forced zero at
$5$ comes with at least one more unit of multiplicity, possibly at $5$
itself.

\begin{proof}
The sums $E$ and $\widehat F$ both live on the $c-1$ nodes
$y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$: writing $G_d=\sum_ia_iy_i^d$ gives
\[
  E_d=\sum_{i=1}^{c-1}a_i(y_i-y_c)y_i^{\,d-1}
      =\sum_{i=1}^{c-1}\beta_iy_i^d,
\]
with $\beta_i=a_i(1-y_c/y_i)$, the $i=c$ term having cancelled.  Hence $\Delta$
is an exponential sum on at most $c-1$ nodes (some coefficients may vanish),
so by the zero bound it is either identically zero or has at most $c-2$ real
zeros counted with multiplicity.

Relabel $A=\{0\}\cup Z'$ as $\alpha_1=0<\alpha_2<\cdots<\alpha_{c-1}=m$; let $r$ be the
number of consecutive pairs $\{\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1}\}=\{d-1,d\}$ in $A$, and
$J=\{j:\alpha_{j+1}\in Z',\ \alpha_j<\alpha_{j+1}-1\}$, so that $|J|=c-2-r$.

By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple zeros $\{0\}\cup Z'$; on a
component $(\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1})$ its sign is $(-1)^{c-1-j}$ because it is positive
past the last zero and flips at each.  We record the zeros of $\Delta$.

First, $\Delta_{m+1}=0$, because $G_m=0$ and $\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat
F_{m+1}$.  Second, if $\alpha_j=\alpha_{j+1}-1$ then $\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1}\in A$, so
$G_{\alpha_j}=G_{\alpha_{j+1}}=0$, and $\alpha_{j+1}\in Z'$, so $\widehat F_{\alpha_{j+1}}=0$;
thus $\Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-E_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-G_{\alpha_{j+1}}+y_cG_{\alpha_{j+1}-1}=0$.  There
are $r$ such forced zeros, one for each consecutive pair.  At every other
marked point $\alpha_{j+1}\in Z'$ one has $\alpha_j<\alpha_{j+1}-1$, so
$\alpha_{j+1}-1\notin A$ and $G_{\alpha_{j+1}-1}\ne0$, and
\[
  \Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-E_{\alpha_{j+1}}=y_cG_{\alpha_{j+1}-1},
  \qquad \sgn\Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=(-1)^{c-1-j}.
\]

If $J=\varnothing$ then $r=c-2$, and the forced zeros together with
$m+1$ give $r+1=c-1>c-2$ zeros, so $\Delta\equiv0$ and the claim is
immediate.  Assume $J\ne\varnothing$ and order $J=\{j_1<\cdots<j_p\}$, so
$p=|J|=c-2-r$.  If $\Delta\equiv0$ the conclusion again holds, so assume
$\Delta\not\equiv0$; the zero bound then caps its zeros at $c-2$.  Let
$N_\Delta$ be the number of zeros of $\Delta$ counted with multiplicity.

Take consecutive $j<j'$ in $J$.  Strictly between
$\alpha_{j+1}$ and $\alpha_{j'+1}$ lie the $q=j'-j-1$ marked points
$\alpha_{j+2},\ldots,\alpha_{j'}$; none of their indices $j+1,\ldots,j'-1$ is
in $J$, so each is preceded by an adjacent marked point and carries a forced
zero; and the endpoint signs
$(-1)^{c-1-j}$, $(-1)^{c-1-j'}$ differ by $(-1)^{q+1}$.  A real-analytic
function, not identically zero, whose endpoint values are nonzero and differ
in sign by $(-1)^{q+1}$ has an odd or even
number of zeros in the open interval according as $q+1$ is odd or even.  So
the number of zeros in $(\alpha_{j+1},\alpha_{j'+1})$, counted with multiplicity, has
the parity of $q+1$ and is at least $q$; hence it is at least $q+1$, one more
than the $q$ forced zeros.  The $r$ forced zeros lie at marked points, and the
parity supplies one additional unit of multiplicity in each of the $p-1$ gaps
between consecutive members of $J$, possibly at a point already forced.  With
the zero at $m+1$ this gives
\[
  N_\Delta\ge r+(p-1)+1=c-2,
\]
which exhausts the zero-multiplicity budget of the $(c-1)$-node sum $\Delta$; in
particular there are no other zeros.

Every one of the $p-1$ extra multiplicities lies strictly below
$\alpha_{j_p+1}$, the gaps being open intervals, so every
zero of $\Delta$ above $\alpha_{j_p+1}$ belongs to the exhaustion just described
and is simple.  There are $c-1-j_p$ of them: the forced zeros at
$\alpha_{j+1}$ for $j_p<j\le c-2$, and the zero at $m+1$.  Since
$\sgn\Delta_{\alpha_{j_p+1}}=(-1)^{c-1-j_p}$ and there is no zero above
$\alpha_{j_p+1}$ but those $c-1-j_p$ simple ones, crossing them flips the sign to
$(-1)^{2(c-1-j_p)}=+1$, and it stays positive.  Thus $\Delta_d\ge0$ for
$d\ge m+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}]
Induct on the number $c-1-\ell$ of displaced zeros.

\emph{Consecutive base case.}  For $Z=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$ this is
Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, which is \eqref{eq:tail} with $\ell=c-1$.  The
standing cutoff $y_c\le1/2$ is not used there.

\emph{Displaced step.}  Suppose $\ell<c-1$ and set $z=\max Z$.  Since $Z$ has
$c-1$ elements and is not $\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$, we have $z\ge c\ge \ell+2$.  Put
$Z'=Z\setminus\{z\}$ and $m=\max Z'$, taking $m=0$ if $Z'$ is empty.  Let $F$ be the sum on $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with $F_0=1$ and
$F|_{Z'}=0$, and $G$ the sum on all $c$ nodes with
$G|_{\{0\}\cup Z'}=0$ and $G_z=1$.  Then
\[
  w=F+\lambda G,\qquad \lambda=-F_z,
\]
because both sides satisfy the same $c$ interpolation conditions.  The set
$Z'$ has the same leading run $\ell$ as $Z$, and $\ell+1\le c-1$, so the induction
hypothesis bounds $\Tail(F)$ by the right side of \eqref{eq:tail}.  It
remains to prove $\Tail(w)<\Tail(F)$.

\emph{Sign comparison.}  By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple zeros
$\{0\}\cup Z'$ and $F$ exactly $Z'$; below $z$ the sum $w$ has the same simple
zeros $Z'$ and no others, and at $z$ it has one additional simple zero.  Write
$\eta=\sgn(F_z)$ and $\mu=|F_z|$, so $w=F-\eta\mu G$.  Since $F$ and $G$ flip
at exactly the same points of $Z'$, their relative sign
$\sgn(F_d/G_d)$ is constant on $(0,\infty)\setminus Z'$, and evaluating at
$d=z$, where $G_z=1$, shows it equals $\eta$; thus $F_d=\eta\sgn(G_d)|F_d|$ and
$G_d=\sgn(G_d)|G_d|$ for $d>0$, whence
\[
  w_d=\eta\sgn(G_d)\bigl(|F_d|-\mu|G_d|\bigr),
  \qquad
  |w_d|=\bigl||F_d|-\mu|G_d|\bigr|.
\]
The sign of $|F_d|-\mu|G_d|$ is therefore that of $\eta\sgn(G_d)w_d$.  Passing
a point of $Z'$ flips both $\sgn w$ and $\sgn G$, leaving this sign unchanged,
so it is constant on $(0,z)\setminus Z'$.  It is positive there: the
continuous function $\varphi(d)=|F_d|-\mu|G_d|$ has $\varphi(0)=1$, because
$w_0=F_0=1$ and $G_0=0$, and since $|w_d|=|\varphi(d)|$ for $d>0$ it has no
zero in $(0,\min Z)$.  At $z$, where $G$ does not vanish, only $\sgn w$
flips, so the sign is negative for all $d>z$.  Hence
\[
  |F_d|>\mu|G_d|\quad(1\le d<z,\ d\notin Z'),
  \qquad
  |F_d|<\mu|G_d|\quad(d>z),
\]
with equality exactly at the zeros of $w$: at the points of $Z'$ both sides
vanish, and at $z$ one has $|F_z|=\mu|G_z|$.

For an exponential sum $V$, write
\[
  \Gamma_V(z)=\frac{\sum_{d\ge z}|V_d|}{|V_z|},
  \qquad \Sigma_G=\sum_{1\le d<z}|G_d|.
\]
Splitting $\Tail(F)-\Tail(w)$ at $z$ and using the two displayed
inequalities, $|F_z|=\mu|G_z|$ and $|G_z|=1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
  \Tail(F)-\Tail(w)
  =\mu\bigl(2\Gamma_F(z)-\Gamma_G(z)+\Sigma_G\bigr).
\end{equation}

\emph{The correction ratio.}  We claim
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
  \Gamma_G(z)\le\frac{\Gamma_F(z)}{1-y_c}\le2\Gamma_F(z).
\end{equation}
Let $\widehat F_d=\eta F_d>0$ for $d>m$ and
$E_d=G_d-y_cG_{d-1}$, an exponential sum on the first $c-1$ nodes.  We first
show
\begin{equation}\label{eq:drive}
  \frac{E_d}{\widehat F_d}\le
  \frac{G_z}{\widehat F_z}\qquad(d\ge z>m).
\end{equation}

Set $A=\{0\}\cup Z'=\{\alpha_1=0<\cdots<\alpha_{c-1}=m\}$,
$\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat F_{m+1}$, and
$\Delta_d=\sigma\widehat F_d-E_d$.  Both $\widehat F$ and $G$ are positive for
$d>m$, the latter because $G_z=1$ with $z>m$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:interlace}
applies and gives $\Delta_d\ge0$ for $d\ge m+1$, that is,
$E_d/\widehat F_d\le\sigma$ for $d\ge m+1$.  Moreover, writing $G_d=\sum_ia_iy_i^d$, the coefficient $a_c$ of the largest
node is positive: $G_d>0$ for $d>m$ forces $a_c\ge0$, and $a_c=0$ would make
$G$ a sum on the $c-1$ nodes $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with the $c-1$ zeros
$\{0\}\cup Z'$, hence identically zero.  Consider the $c$-node sum
\[
  \Psi_d=\widehat F_{m+1}G_d-G_{m+1}\widehat F_d .
\]
Its $y_c$-coefficient is $\widehat F_{m+1}a_c>0$, so it is not identically
zero and $\Psi_d\sim\widehat F_{m+1}a_cy_c^{\,d}>0$ as $d\to\infty$; it
vanishes on $Z'\cup\{m+1\}$, which is $c-1$ points, so by the zero bound
those are its only zeros and $\Psi$ is positive beyond $m+1$.  Hence
$G_d/\widehat F_d>\sigma$ for $d>m+1$, while $G_z/\widehat F_z=\sigma$ when
$z=m+1$; in either case $\sigma\le G_z/\widehat F_z$.  Therefore
\[
  \frac{E_d}{\widehat F_d}\le\sigma\le\frac{G_z}{\widehat F_z}
  \qquad(d\ge z>m),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:drive}.

Induction in $G_d=y_cG_{d-1}+E_d$, with $G_z=1$ and
\eqref{eq:drive}, gives for $t\ge0$
\[
  \frac{G_{z+t}}{G_z}
  \le\sum_{j=0}^t y_c^{t-j}
       \frac{\widehat F_{z+j}}{\widehat F_z}.
\]
Summing over $t$ (a geometric series in $y_c$) proves the first inequality
in \eqref{eq:gamma}; the second uses $y_c\le1/2$.

\emph{Strictness.}  It remains to see that $\Sigma_G>0$, so that
\eqref{eq:split} is positive.  By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple
zeros $\{0\}\cup Z'$ and none other.  If $z>m+1$, then $m+1<z$ and
$m+1\notin\{0\}\cup Z'$, so $G_{m+1}\ne0$ and $\Sigma_G>0$.  If $z=m+1$, then
$|Z'|=c-2<z-1$ because $z\ge c$, so some $d$ with $1\le d<z$ avoids
$\{0\}\cup Z'$, and again $\Sigma_G>0$.  So the
difference in \eqref{eq:split} is strictly positive, and the one-step
comparison
\[
  \Tail(w)<\Tail(F)
\]
holds.  This is the deletion step, and it is strict: the tail of the $c$-node
sum $w$ is smaller than that of the $(c-1)$-node sum $F$, which keeps the
lower zeros $Z'$ but drops the largest prescribed zero and the largest node.
This completes the induction, and equality in \eqref{eq:tail} can occur only
in the base case.
\end{proof}

The restriction $\rho_i\ge2$ matters.  For $c=2$,
$y_1=1/10$, $y_2=4/5$, and $Z=\{2\}$, the normalized coefficients are
$a_1=64/63$ and $a_2=-1/63$, so
$\Tail(w)=52/405>1/9=y_1/(1-y_1)$: \eqref{eq:tail} fails once a node
passes the cutoff.  The cutoff enters the proof at two places only, through
$1/(1-y_c)\le2$ in \eqref{eq:gamma} and through the factors discarded in
Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} below; whether $2$ is the true threshold we have not
determined.

\section{Coefficient order statistics}

Let
\[
 P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i),\qquad 2\le r_1<\cdots<r_L,
\]
with the roots distinct for the lemma below (repetitions are handled in
Theorem~\ref{thm:order}), and let $F(x)=\sum_{j=0}^Df_jx^j\in\R[x]$ be a
nonzero multiple of $P$.  As in the introduction, $b_1\ge b_2\ge\cdots$ are
the magnitudes $|f_j|$, $j<D$, in decreasing order.

\begin{lemma}[Certificate with excluded positions]\label{lem:slack}
Assume the roots $2\le r_1<\cdots<r_L$ are distinct and $|f_D|=1$.  Fix $0\le u\le L-1$, with $r_1>1$
in place of $r_1\ge2$ permitted when $u=0$.  For every set $U$ of $u$
coefficient positions below $D$, some $j<D$ outside $U$ satisfies
\[
 |f_j|\ge\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use all $c=L$ roots $\rho_1=r_L>\rho_2=r_{L-1}>\cdots>\rho_c=r_1$.  A
position $j$ has backward distance $d=D-j$.  Let $W\subseteq\N_{>0}$ be the
set of distances of the positions in $U$, and let $w$ be the certificate on
these $c$ nodes, with $w_0=1$ and with zero set $Z$ consisting of $W$
together with the first $L-u-1$ positive distances outside $W$.  The $u$
positions have distinct distances, so $|W|=u$, and $L-u-1\ge0$ because
$u\le L-1$; hence $|Z|=c-1$, as required; write $w_d=\sum_i a_iy_i^d$.

The dual-certificate identity is explicit.  Since $F(\rho_i)=0$ for the $c$
chosen roots,
\[
  0=\sum_{i=1}^c a_i\rho_i^{-D}F(\rho_i)
   =\sum_{j\le D}f_jw_{D-j}
   =f_D+\sum_{j<D}f_jw_{D-j},
\]
using $w_0=1$.  The terms with $j\in U$ vanish because $D-j\in W\subseteq Z$.
Hence, as $|f_D|=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual}
  1\le\sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|f_j|\,|w_{D-j}|
  \le\Bigl(\max_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|f_j|\Bigr)
        \sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|w_{D-j}|.
\end{equation}

The set $Z$ contains $\{1,\ldots,L-u-1\}$: a distance $t\le L-u-1$ is in
$W\subseteq Z$ if occupied by $U$, and otherwise is among the first
$L-u-1$ distances outside $W$.  Thus the leading run $\ell$ of $Z$ is at least
$L-u-1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} bounds the tail by the product of
$y_i/(1-y_i)$ over the $\ell+1\ge L-u$ smallest reciprocal nodes $y_1,\ldots,y_{\ell+1}$.  Every
such factor is at most $1$, since $y_i\le1/2$ is exactly $\rho_i\ge2$, so
discarding all but the first $L-u$ factors only weakens the bound: the tail is
at most the product over the $L-u$ smallest reciprocal nodes.  These are
$\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_{L-u}$, that is,
$r_L,r_{L-1},\ldots,r_{u+1}$; the last index is
$L-(L-u)+1=u+1$, which is why the product below starts there.  For $u=0$
nothing is discarded: there $Z=\{1,\ldots,L-1\}$, so $\Tail(w)$ is evaluated
exactly by Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, which needs only $y_i<1$, and the
bound holds already for $r_1>1$.  Therefore
\[
  \sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|w_{D-j}|
  \le\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|=\Tail(w)
  \le\prod_{i=1}^{L-u}\frac{\rho_i^{-1}}{1-\rho_i^{-1}}
  =\frac{1}{\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)}.
\]
Substituting into \eqref{eq:dual} gives
$1\le\bigl(\max_{j<D,j\notin U}|f_j|\bigr)/\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)$, which is
the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Coefficient order statistics]\label{thm:order}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ be real with $r_1\ge2$, repetitions allowed, and
let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.  Then for every
$0\le u\le L-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
  b_{u+1}\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1).
\end{equation}
If instead only $r_1>1$ is assumed, \eqref{eq:order} still holds for $u=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume first that the $r_i$ are distinct and $|f_D|=1$.  The assertion that
some $j<D$ outside an arbitrary set $U$ of $u$ positions has $|f_j|\ge T$ is
equivalent to the assertion that at least $u+1$ positions $j<D$ have
$|f_j|\ge T$, that is, to $b_{u+1}\ge T$: any $U$ of size $u$ misses one of
the $u+1$ large positions, and conversely the $u$ largest positions form a set
$U$ that the $(u+1)$-st largest avoids.  Apply this with
$T=\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)$ to the conclusion of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}.

For general $f_D\ne0$, the quotient $F/f_D$ has leading coefficient $1$ and
coefficient magnitudes $b_k(F)/|f_D|$, so the normalized case gives
$b_k(F)\ge|f_D|\,T$ for every $k$.

For repetitions, put $r_{i,\varepsilon}=r_i+i\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon>0$, so that $2\le r_{1,\varepsilon}<\cdots<r_{L,\varepsilon}$ and
$r_{i,\varepsilon}\to r_i$, and write $F=Q\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ and
$F_\varepsilon=Q\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_{i,\varepsilon})$ with the same $Q$.  Then
$\deg F_\varepsilon=D$, the leading coefficient is still $f_D$, and each
coefficient of $F_\varepsilon$ is a polynomial in $\varepsilon$, so the
coefficient vector of $F_\varepsilon$ tends to that of $F$; an order statistic of a converging tuple is continuous, so
$b_{u+1}(F_\varepsilon)\to b_{u+1}(F)$.  The distinct case gives
$b_{u+1}(F_\varepsilon)\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_{i,\varepsilon}-1)$, and letting
$\varepsilon\to0$ proves \eqref{eq:order}.

The $u=0$ clause follows from the $u=0$ clause of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} by
the same perturbation, which keeps $r_{i,\varepsilon}>1$.
\end{proof}

With $k=u+1$, \eqref{eq:order} reads
$b_k\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$ for $1\le k\le L$, the form used in the
introduction and in Corollary~\ref{cor:complex}.

\begin{corollary}[Complex multiples]\label{cor:complex}
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} holds verbatim for a nonzero $F\in\mathbb C[x]$
divisible by $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $g=F/f_D=PQ$ with $Q\in\mathbb C[x]$ monic.  Since $P$ is real,
$\operatorname{Re}g=P\operatorname{Re}Q$ is a monic real multiple of $P$ of
the same degree $D$, and $|\operatorname{Re}g_j|\le|g_j|$ for every $j$.
Coefficientwise domination passes to order statistics, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} applied to $\operatorname{Re}g$ gives
$b_k(g)\ge b_k(\operatorname{Re}g)\ge\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$; multiplying
through by $|f_D|$ gives \eqref{eq:order} for $F$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Logarithmic mass]\label{cor:mass}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ be real with $r_1\ge2$, repetitions allowed, and
let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass}
 \Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each product in \eqref{eq:order} is at least one because $r_i\ge2$, so the
$L$ largest nonleading magnitudes are nonzero, as is $f_D$; since $D\ge L$
these are $L+1$ distinct positions.  Summing $\log^+\ge\log$ over them alone,
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\log|f_D|+\sum_{u=0}^{L-1}\log b_{u+1}
 \ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1),
\]
using $\log b_{u+1}\ge\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=u+1}^L\log(r_i-1)$ and collecting the
index-$i$ terms, which occur exactly $i$ times.
\end{proof}

At the bottom of the allowed range the two bounds behave differently.  For
$r_1=\cdots=r_L=2$ every product in \eqref{eq:order} equals $1$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} still forces $L$ nonleading coefficients of
magnitude at least $|f_D|$, while \eqref{eq:mass} reduces to
$\Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|$ and says nothing more.  The order-statistic
bound stays informative over the whole root range; the mass bound does not.
Section~\ref{sec:sharp} shows that the collapse is in the bound and not in
$\Lambda$: Propositions~\ref{prop:unbounded}, \ref{prop:neartwo} and
\ref{prop:quadratic} recover, at $r_i=2$, first $b_1\ge L$, then
$b_2\ge L-1$, and finally a mass of order $L^2$.

\begin{corollary}[Prime roots]\label{cor:prime}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ with $r_i\ge p_i$, the $i$th prime, and let $F$ be
a nonzero multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ with $|f_D|\ge1$.  Then
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\Bigl(\frac12+o(1)\Bigr)L^2\log L .
\]
In particular $\Lambda(F)=\Omega(L^2\log L)$, and the bound is asymptotically
attained: for $F=P_L=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-p_i)$,
$\Lambda(P_L)\le(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $p_i$ for the $i$th prime, so $r_i\ge p_i\sim i\log i$ and, since
$r_i-1\ge p_i-1$,
$\log(r_i-1)\ge\log p_i-O(1/p_i)$.  Drop the nonnegative term
$(L+1)\log|f_D|$ from \eqref{eq:mass}.  Hence
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\sum_{i=1}^L i\log(r_i-1)
 \ge\sum_{i=2}^L i\bigl(\log i+\log\log i+o(1)\bigr),
\]
the $i=1$ term being nonnegative, and
$\sum_{i\le L}i\log i=(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$, so the remaining terms
are $o(L^2\log L)$.  This is $\bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\bigr)L^2\log L$.

For the matching upper bound, the nonleading coefficients of $P_L$ are the
elementary symmetric sums $e_k(p_1,\ldots,p_L)\le\binom{L}{k}p_L^k$, so
\[
 \Lambda(P_L)\le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom{L}{k}
 +\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L
 \le L^2\log2+\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L,
\]
and $\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L=(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$ while
$L^2\log2=o(L^2\log L)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sharp prime-root infimum]\label{cor:infimum}
Let $P_L=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-p_i)$ and
\[
  M_L=\inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}\Lambda(QP_L).
\]
Then
\[
  M_L=\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} with $r_i=p_i$ and $|f_D|=1$ gives, for every monic
multiple $F$ of $P_L$,
\[
  \Lambda(F)\ge\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log(p_i-1)
  =\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2),
\]
the $i=1$ term being zero.  The equality holds because
$i\log i<p_i<i(\log i+\log\log i)$ for $i\ge6$~\cite{rosser-schoenfeld}
gives $\log(p_i-1)=\log i+\log\log i+O(1)$ for $i\ge2$, while
$\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log i=\frac12L^2\log L+O(L^2)$ and
$\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log\log i=\frac12L^2\log\log L+o(L^2)$.  The matching upper
bound is the one for $P_L$ from Corollary~\ref{cor:prime},
\[
  \Lambda(P_L)\le L^2\log2+\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L
  =\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2).
\]
\end{proof}

Multiplying by another factor can lower the mass, so $P_L$ need not
minimize it, even for small $L$.  For example, $P_2=(x-2)(x-3)=x^2-5x+6$ has
$\Lambda(P_2)=\log30$, while $(x+1)P_2=x^3-4x^2+x+6$ has $\Lambda=\log24$,
since coefficients of magnitude one contribute nothing.
Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum} describes the infimum $M_L$ asymptotically; it
does not say that $P_L$ attains it for any finite $L$.

\begin{corollary}[Near-optimality of the root product]\label{cor:near}
Let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed,
$P=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$, and
\[
  M(P)=\inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}\Lambda(PQ).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:near}
  0\le\Lambda(P)-M(P)
  \le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom Lk
    +\sum_{i=1}^L i\log\frac{r_i}{r_i-1}
  =O(L^2),
\end{equation}
with an absolute implied constant.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Taking $Q=1$ gives $M(P)\le\Lambda(P)$, while Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} gives
$M(P)\ge\sum_{i=1}^L i\log(r_i-1)$, because a monic multiple has $|f_D|=1$.
In the other direction the nonleading coefficients of $P$ are the elementary
symmetric sums, and each $k$-fold product of roots is at most the product of
the $k$ largest, so
\[
  e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)\le\binom Lk\prod_{i=L-k+1}^{L}r_i .
\]
Since $e_k\ge\binom Lk2^k>1$, summing logarithms gives
\[
  \Lambda(P)=\sum_{k=1}^L\log e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)
  \le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom Lk+\sum_{i=1}^L i\log r_i ,
\]
the sum over $k$ collecting $\log r_i$ exactly $i$ times.
Subtracting the lower bound and using
$\log r_i-\log(r_i-1)=\log\frac{r_i}{r_i-1}$ gives \eqref{eq:near}; finally
$\binom Lk\le2^L$ and $r_i/(r_i-1)\le2$ because $r_i\ge2$, so the right side
is at most $(L^2+\frac{L(L+1)}2)\log2=O(L^2)$.
\end{proof}

In words, no monic multiplier lowers the mass of the root product by more
than $O(L^2)$.  So whenever $\Lambda(P)$ grows faster than $L^2$, as for the
prime-root product of Corollary~\ref{cor:prime} with
$\Lambda(P_L)\sim\frac12L^2\log L$, the product $P$ is asymptotically optimal
among monic multiples.  For the first $L$ primes, Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum}
gives the sharp form of this statement.

\section{Sharpness}\label{sec:sharp}

How close is Theorem~\ref{thm:order} to optimal?  This section treats the
first row, then the bottom of the root range, where \eqref{eq:mass}
degenerates, and finally the rows $k\ge2$.

\subsection{The first row}

Row $k=1$ is $b_1\ge|P(1)|$.  Already for $L=1$ this bound is approached but
never attained: in $F=(x-r)\sum_{k\le m}c_kx^k$ with $c_m=1$, every $|f_j|\le r-1$
would force $c_{j-1}=rc_j+f_j\ge rc_j-(r-1)$, hence $c_j\ge1$ downward from
$c_m$ and $|f_0|=r|c_0|\ge r$.  It is approached: taking
$c_j=1+\varepsilon-\varepsilon r^{m-j}$ with $\varepsilon=1/(r^m-1)$ gives
$c_0=0$ and $|f_j|=(r-1)(1+\varepsilon)$ for $1\le j\le m$, so $b_1\to r-1$
as $m\to\infty$.

Nor is row $k=1$ the best bound the certificate method gives.  The
consecutive certificate, which Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} uses when $u=0$, need
not have the smallest tail at fixed nodes (see the example after
Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}).  Take $P=(x-2)(x-3)$,
nodes $\rho=(3,2)$ and $Z=\{2\}$, whose tail is $3/10$ by the example after
Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}.  Then \eqref{eq:dual}
with $U=\varnothing$ gives
\[
  b_1\ge10/3
\]
for every monic multiple, compared with $|P(1)|=2$.  Corollary~8
of~\cite{dj}, a closed formula for the reduced height of $(x-a)(x-b)$,
evaluates at $(a,b)=(3,2)$ to $10/3$, so here the row is off by a factor of
$5/3$.

At these nodes $Z=\{2\}$ is in fact the best choice, so the certificate
method loses nothing at $(2,3)$: minimizing $\Tail(w)$ over all
$Z\subset\{1,\ldots,14\}$ with $|Z|=1$ returns $Z=\{2\}$ and the bound
$10/3$, which is the reduced height.  The optimum need not be displaced---at
nodes $(5,3)$ the consecutive set is already optimal---and the gain is
modest.  Minimizing the tail over all $Z$ in the same range gives, against
$|P(1)|$, the ratios
\[
  \begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
    \text{roots} & (2,3) & (2,5) & (3,5) & (2,3,5) & (2,3,7) & (3,5,7) & (2,3,5,7)\\\hline
    \text{ratio} & \tfrac53 & \tfrac75 & 1 & \tfrac{31}{17} & \tfrac{41}{23}
      & \tfrac{71}{62} & \tfrac{39799}{21337}
  \end{array}
\]
all below $2$.  These minima were computed in exact rational arithmetic, over
all $Z$ of the required size contained in $\{1,\ldots,R\}$ with $R=14$ for
$L=2$, $R=11$ for $L=3$ and $R=10$ for $L=4$; in each case the minimizing
$Z$ lies well inside that range and is unchanged when $R$ is enlarged to
$30$, $18$ and $14$ respectively, the tails increasing again for large
$\max Z$.

The ratios above stay below $2$, but that is a feature of these small root
sets and not a general phenomenon.

\begin{proposition}[Unbounded looseness of the row $k=1$]
\label{prop:unbounded}
Let $1<r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed, and put
$B=\prod_{i=1}^L(r_i-1)$ and $S=\sum_{i=1}^Lr_i$.  Then every monic multiple
$F$ of $\prod_i(x-r_i)$ satisfies
\[
  b_1(F)\ \ge\ \frac{SB}{B+1},
\]
whose ratio to the value $B=|P(1)|$ given by \eqref{eq:order} at $u=0$ is
$S/(B+1)$.  If moreover $r_i\ge2$ for every $i$, then $B\ge1$ and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:halfsum}
  b_1(F)\ \ge\ \frac S2=\frac{r_1+\cdots+r_L}2 .
\end{equation}
Neither \eqref{eq:halfsum} nor the row $u=0$ of \eqref{eq:order} implies the
other.  For a general nonzero leading coefficient both right sides acquire a
factor $|f_D|$, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  For $r_i=2+iL^{-3}$ the ratio $S/(B+1)$ is $\sim L$, so the ratio
between a certificate bound and $|P(1)|$ is unbounded in $L$, already
for distinct roots.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume first that the roots are distinct.  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$, of size
$L-1$, and let $w$ be the certificate on the
nodes $y_i=r_i^{-1}$, so $w_0=1$ and $w_2=\cdots=w_L=0$.  Write
$W(t)=\sum_{d\ge0}w_dt^d=N(t)/\Pi(t)$ with $\Pi(t)=\prod_i(1-y_it)$ and
$\deg N\le L-1$.  Then $N(t)-(1+w_1t)\Pi(t)$ has degree at most $L+1$ and is
divisible by $t^{L+1}$, hence equals $\kappa t^{L+1}$, so
\[
  W(t)=1+w_1t+\frac{\kappa t^{L+1}}{\Pi(t)} .
\]
The coefficient of $t^L$ in $N$ vanishes, which reads
\[
  (-1)^Le_L(y)+w_1(-1)^{L-1}e_{L-1}(y)=0,
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  e_{L-1}(y)=e_L(y)\sum_{i}y_i^{-1}=e_L(y)\,S,
\]
so that $w_1=1/S$.  Then
$\kappa=-w_1[t^L]\Pi(t)=(-1)^{L+1}e_L(y)/S$, and $\Pi(t)=e_L(y)\prod_i(r_i-t)$,
so the last term is $(-1)^{L+1}t^{L+1}/\bigl(S\prod_i(r_i-t)\bigr)$.
Expanding $1/\Pi(t)=\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)t^j$ gives
$w_{L+1+j}=(-1)^{L+1}e_L(y)h_j(y)/S$, which is one-signed, so
\[
  \Tail(w)=w_1+\frac{e_L(y)}S\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)
  =\frac1S+\frac1S\prod_{i}\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}
  =\frac{1+B^{-1}}S .
\]
The dual-certificate identity in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}, applied
with $U=\varnothing$ and this $w$, gives $b_1(F)\ge1/\Tail(w)=SB/(B+1)$.  The
computation used only $0<y_i<1$, that is $r_i>1$.

For repetitions, perturb as in Theorem~\ref{thm:order}: the roots
$r_{i,\varepsilon}=r_i+i\varepsilon$ are distinct and exceed $1$, the
coefficient vector of $F_\varepsilon$ tends to that of $F$ so
$b_1(F_\varepsilon)\to b_1(F)$, and $B$ and $S$ are continuous in the roots.
Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives the bound in general.

If every $r_i\ge2$ then each $r_i-1\ge1$, so $B\ge1$; as $B\mapsto B/(B+1)$
increases, $B/(B+1)\ge\tfrac12$ and \eqref{eq:halfsum} follows.  Neither
bound dominates: at $r=(3,5)$ one has $S/2=4<8=B$, while at $r=(2,\ldots,2)$
one has $S/2=L>1=B$.

Finally, for $r_i=2+iL^{-3}$ one has
$\log B=\sum_i\log(1+iL^{-3})=O(L^{-1})$, so $B\to1$, while
$S=2L+O(L^{-1})$; hence $S/(B+1)\sim L$.
\end{proof}

At those roots \eqref{eq:order} gives $b_1\ge B\to1$, which is nearly vacuous,
while the certificate above gives $b_1\gtrsim L$: the product bound collapses
as the roots approach $2$, but the number of roots still forces a large
coefficient.  The zero set $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$ used here is not always
optimal---at $(2,3,5)$ it gives $80/9$, against $248/17$ for
$Z=\{1,3\}$---but it suffices to settle the boundedness question.

\subsection{The bottom of the range}

At $r_i=2$ the certificate of Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded} gives
$b_1\ge L$, where the row $u=0$ of \eqref{eq:order} gives only $1$.  A
different choice of zero set reaches the second coefficient as well.

\begin{proposition}[Two large coefficients at the bottom of the range]
\label{prop:neartwo}
Let $L\ge2$.  Every monic multiple $F$ of $(x-2)^L$ satisfies
\[
  b_1(F)\ge L,\qquad b_2(F)\ge L-1,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \Lambda(F)\ge\log\bigl(L(L-1)\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the perturbation of Theorem~\ref{thm:order} it suffices to bound $b_1$ and
$b_2$ for distinct roots $2<r_1<\cdots<r_L$ and let every $r_i\to2$.  The
first bound is \eqref{eq:halfsum}, whose right side $S/2$ tends to $L$.

For the second, let $z$ be the backward distance of a position attaining
$b_1(F)$ and apply the identity of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} with $U$ that single
position: any certificate $w$ with $w_z=0$ gives $1\le b_2(F)\Tail(w)$.  Write
$e_k(r)=e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)$ and $y_i=r_i^{-1}$, so $e_1(r)=S$,
$e_L(r)=\prod_ir_i$, $e_L(y)=1/e_L(r)$, $e_{L-1}(y)=S/e_L(r)$, $e_{L-2}(y)=e_2(r)/e_L(r)$
and $\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)=\prod_i(1-y_i)^{-1}=e_L(r)/B$.

\emph{Case $z=1$.}  Take $Z=\{1,\ldots,L\}\setminus\{2\}$, so $w_0=1$,
$w_1=w_3=\cdots=w_L=0$ and $w_2$ is free.  As in
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}, $\bigl(W(t)-(1+w_2t^2)\bigr)\Pi(t)$ is
divisible by $t^{L+1}$ and has degree at most $L+2$, so
\[
  W(t)=1+w_2t^2+\frac{(\kappa+\lambda t)\,t^{L+1}}{\Pi(t)} .
\]
Comparing the coefficients of $t^L$, $t^{L+1}$ and $t^{L+2}$ gives
\[
  w_2=-\frac{e_L(y)}{e_{L-2}(y)},\qquad
  \kappa=w_2(-1)^Le_{L-1}(y),\qquad
  \lambda=-w_2(-1)^Le_L(y),
\]
so that $w_d=w_2(-1)^L\bigl(e_{L-1}(y)h_{d-L-1}(y)-e_L(y)h_{d-L-2}(y)\bigr)$
for $d\ge L+1$.  These terms are one-signed: $h_j\ge y_Lh_{j-1}$, since
multiplying each monomial of degree $j-1$ by $y_L$ gives distinct monomials
of degree $j$, while $e_{L-1}(y)=\sum_ie_L(y)/y_i\ge e_L(y)/y_L$.  Summing,
\[
  \Tail(w)=|w_2|\Bigl(1+\Bigl[\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)\Bigr]
                       \bigl(e_{L-1}(y)-e_L(y)\bigr)\Bigr)
  =\frac1{e_2(r)}\Bigl(1+\frac{S-1}B\Bigr)
  =\frac{B+S-1}{e_2(r)\,B} .
\]
As every $r_i\to2$ we have $B\to1$, $S\to2L$ and $e_2(r)\to2L(L-1)$, so
$\Tail(w)\to1/(L-1)$.

\emph{Case $2\le z\le L$.}  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}, $\Tail(w)=(1+B^{-1})/S\to1/L$.

\emph{Case $z>L$.}  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L-1\}\cup\{z\}$, whose largest element
is $z$.  The deletion step in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} removes $z$
together with the largest reciprocal node, leaving exactly the certificate of
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded} on the $L-1$ largest roots; so
$\Tail(w)<(1+B'^{-1})/S'$, where $B'\to1$ and $S'\to2(L-1)$, and the limit is
again $1/(L-1)$.

The three bounds hold for every $\varepsilon$ and none of the three tails
depends on $z$, so for each $\varepsilon$ the multiple $F_\varepsilon$
satisfies $b_2(F_\varepsilon)\ge1/T_\varepsilon$, where $T_\varepsilon$ is
the largest of the three tails.  Since $T_\varepsilon\to1/(L-1)$
and $b_2(F_\varepsilon)\to b_2(F)$, letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives
$b_2(F)\ge L-1$.  Finally $b_1$ and $b_2$ are attained at distinct positions
and $L-1\ge1$, so $\Lambda(F)\ge\log L+\log(L-1)$.
\end{proof}

Far more is true, and at every root set, not only at $r_i=2$: the infimum of the mass over monic multiples is of order $L^2$
throughout the range where \eqref{eq:mass} degenerates.  The proof uses
Jensen's formula rather than a certificate.  It is close in spirit to the
work of Beaucoup, Borwein, Boyd and Pinner on how high a zero a power series
with constant term $1$ and bounded coefficients can have at a prescribed
point~\cite{bbbp-multiple,bbbp-ray}; the difference is that $k-1$ coefficients
are exempted here rather than all being bounded.

\begin{proposition}[Quadratic mass at every root set]
\label{prop:quadratic}
Let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed.  Every monic real
multiple $F$ of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ satisfies, for every
$\tfrac12<\rho<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jensen}
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ \frac{(2\rho)^{L-k+1}-1}{(1-\rho)^{-k}-1},\qquad 1\le k\le L,
\end{equation}
and hence $\Lambda(F)\ge L^2/80$ for $L\ge8$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $D=\deg F$ and let $A(t)=t^DF(1/t)=\sum_{d=0}^Df_{D-d}t^d$ be the
reciprocal polynomial, so $A(0)=f_D=1$, while $A(1/r_i)=r_i^{-D}F(r_i)=0$, so
$A$ has at least $L$ zeros in $(0,\tfrac12]$ counted with multiplicity,
namely the $1/r_i$.

For a real polynomial $h$ and an integer $z\ge1$ put $T_zh=h-(t/z)h'$, so that
$[t^d](T_zh)=(1-d/z)[t^d]h$: the operator annihilates the coefficient at
$t^z$ and fixes the constant term.  It also satisfies
\[
  T_zh=-\frac{t^{z+1}}z\bigl(t^{-z}h\bigr)' .
\]
If $h$ has $m$ zeros in an interval $I\subset(0,\infty)$, counted with
multiplicity, then so does $t^{-z}h$, and by Rolle's theorem its derivative
has at least $m-1$ zeros in the convex hull of them, hence in $I$; the factor
$-t^{z+1}/z$ contributes no zero in $I$.  So $T_zh$ retains at least $m-1$
zeros in $I$.

Fix $k$ and let $z_1<\cdots<z_{k-1}$ be the backward distances $D-j$ of the
positions $j<D$ carrying the $k-1$ largest nonleading magnitudes.  Put
$g=T_{z_{k-1}}\cdots T_{z_1}A$, so that
\[
  [t^d]g=[t^d]A\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(1-\frac d{z_i}\Bigr).
\]
Then $g(0)=1$, the coefficients at $d=z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1}$ vanish, and by the
previous paragraph $g$ has at least $L-k+1$ zeros in $(0,\tfrac12]$.

If $d\ge1$ and $d\notin\{z_i\}$ then $|[t^d]A|=|f_{D-d}|\le b_k(F)$, that
position not being among the $k-1$ largest.  The $z_i$ are distinct positive
integers, so $z_i\ge i$ and
\[
  \prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl|1-\frac d{z_i}\Bigr|
  \le\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(1+\frac di\Bigr)=\binom{d+k-1}{k-1} .
\]
Hence $|[t^d]g|\le b_k(F)\binom{d+k-1}{k-1}$ for $d\ge1$, and since
$\sum_{d\ge0}\binom{d+k-1}{k-1}x^d=(1-x)^{-k}$, taking $x=\rho$ gives
\[
  \max_{|t|=\rho}|g(t)|\ \le\ 1+b_k(F)\bigl((1-\rho)^{-k}-1\bigr).
\]
Jensen's formula for $g$ on $|t|\le\rho$, with $g(0)=1$ and at least
$L-k+1$ zeros of modulus at most $\tfrac12$, gives
$(2\rho)^{L-k+1}\le\max_{|t|=\rho}|g|$, which is \eqref{eq:jensen}.

Now take $\rho=\tfrac9{10}$, let $L\ge8$ and $1\le k\le\lfloor L/8\rfloor$.  Then
$(9/5)^{L-k+1}-1\ge(9/5)^{7L/8}\bigl(1-(9/5)^{-7L/8}\bigr)$ and
$10^k-1\le10^{L/8}$, so
\[
  \log b_k(F)\ \ge\ \tfrac78L\log\tfrac95-\tfrac18L\log10
    +\log\bigl(1-(9/5)^{-7L/8}\bigr)\ \ge\ \tfrac L5 ,
\]
the numerical values being $\tfrac78\log\tfrac95=0.5143\ldots$ and
$\tfrac18\log10=0.2878\ldots$, and the last term at least $-0.017$ for
$L\ge8$.  These $\lfloor L/8\rfloor$ magnitudes sit at distinct positions and
exceed $1$, and $\lfloor L/8\rfloor\ge L/16$, so
$\Lambda(F)\ge(L/16)(L/5)=L^2/80$.
\end{proof}

Combining this with the certificate bound closes the mass problem to within
absolute constants over the whole root range.

\begin{corollary}[The mass infimum at every root set]\label{cor:allroots}
Let $L\ge2$, let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, put $P=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ and
$M(P)=\inf\{\Lambda(PQ):Q\in\R[x]\text{ monic}\}$.  Then
\[
  M(P)\ \asymp\ L^2+\sum_{i=1}^Li\log(r_i-1),
\]
with absolute implied constants.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the lower bound, Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic} gives
$M(P)\ge L^2/80$ when $L\ge8$, and \eqref{eq:halfsum} gives
$b_1\ge L\ge2$, hence $M(P)\ge\log2\ge L^2\log2/49$, when $2\le L\le7$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} with $|f_D|=1$ gives
$M(P)\ge\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$, so $M(P)$ is at least a constant multiple of
the sum of the two terms.
For the upper bound take $Q=1$: as in Corollary~\ref{cor:near},
$\Lambda(P)\le\sum_k\log\binom Lk+\sum_ii\log r_i$, and
$\sum_ii\log r_i\le\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)+\tfrac{L(L+1)}2\log2$ because
$r_i/(r_i-1)\le2$, so
$\Lambda(P)\le2L^2\log2+\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$.
\end{proof}

The two terms take turns.  For the first $L$ primes the sum dominates and
Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum} gives the sharp constant; at $r_i=2$ the sum
vanishes and the $L^2$ term is everything.

Three remarks on \eqref{eq:jensen}.  First, it is useful only while
$(1-\rho)^{-k}$ has not overtaken $(2\rho)^{L-k+1}$, that is for $k$ up to a
constant multiple of $L$; it says nothing about the last rows.  In the first
rows, by contrast, Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} remains the stronger
statement for moderate $L$: row $k=1$ of \eqref{eq:jensen} at
$\rho=\tfrac9{10}$ reads $b_1\ge\bigl((9/5)^L-1\bigr)/9$, which overtakes
the bound $b_1\ge L$ of \eqref{eq:halfsum} only at $L=8$ and is exponential
thereafter.  Second, the constant $1/80$ comes from one radius
and one cutoff $k\le L/8$, chosen for clean arithmetic; a sharper choice
would improve it, and we have not pursued the best value.

Third, the numerical experiments reported below suggest the clean per-row
statement
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ L-k+1\qquad(1\le k\le L)
\]
for every monic multiple of $(x-2)^L$, interpolating between $b_1\ge L$ and
the trivial $b_L\ge1$.  The first rows are already covered: for
$k\le L/8$ the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic} gives
$b_k\ge e^{L/5}$, which exceeds $L$ once $L\ge13$;
Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} covers $k=1,2$ and Theorem~\ref{thm:order}
the row $k=L$.  What remains open are the rows $L/8<k\le L-1$, and
$3\le k\le L-1$ for $L\le12$; the consequence
$\Lambda(F)\ge\log L!$ is in any case asymptotically weaker than
Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic}, so the interest is in the individual rows.
The evidence is negative only, but it is exact over a family rather than
sampled.  For fixed $L$, fixed $\deg Q$ and a fixed set of $k-1$ exempt
positions, minimizing the largest remaining coefficient magnitude over monic
$Q$ is a linear program, and the minimum of these over all exempt sets is the
infimum of $b_k$ over monic $Q$ of that degree.  We solved every such program
for $L\le7$, $\deg Q\le7$ and $1\le k\le L$, reconstructing each minimizer
and recomputing its order statistics.  No minimum fell below $L-k+1$, and
in every case the smallest value occurred at the largest degree tried, so
the bounded-degree values need not be the infimum over all degrees.  A
search over bounded degree cannot confirm a lower bound; it can only fail to
refute one.

\subsection{The rows $k\ge2$}

What Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} buys is a bound in closed product form that is
uniform in the excluded set, and that is what the rows $k\ge2$ need: for
$u\ge1$ the set $U$ is arbitrary, so the distances $W$ on which the
certificate must vanish are forced, and only the remaining $L-u-1$ zeros are
ours to place.  Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} places them at the first available
distances.  That choice is convenient, not optimal, and the free zeros may be
placed anywhere; the resulting bound is then the worst case over $U$ and has
no closed form.  Proposition~\ref{prop:nonsharp} below carries out this
optimization for a single root set and improves \eqref{eq:order} there, which
shows that the rows $k\ge2$ are not sharp in general.  They can, however,
be sharp.

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of the row $k=2$ at $(2,3)$]
\label{prop:sharp23}
For $n\ge2$ put
\[
  t_n=\frac{2}{1-2^{1-n}+3^{-n}},
  \qquad a_n=2+t_n\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr),
  \qquad
  F_n(x)=x^{n+1}-a_nx^n+t_n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^j .
\]
Then each $F_n$ is a monic multiple of $(x-2)(x-3)$ with $b_2(F_n)=t_n$, and
$t_n$ decreases to $2$.  Hence
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}
  b_2\bigl(Q(x)(x-2)(x-3)\bigr)=2,
\]
the value \eqref{eq:order} asserts for $u=1$.  Every $F_n$ has $b_2>2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Summing the geometric progression, $F_n(2)=0$ and $F_n(3)=0$ read
$a_n=2+t_n(1-2^{-n})$ and $a_n=3+\tfrac12t_n(1-3^{-n})$; the two agree exactly
when $t_n\bigl(\tfrac12-2^{-n}+\tfrac123^{-n}\bigr)=1$, which is the stated
$t_n$.  Writing $d_n=1-2^{1-n}+3^{-n}$, one has $d_n<1$ because
$3^{-n}<2\cdot2^{-n}$, and $d_n\ge\tfrac12$ because $2^{1-n}\le\tfrac12$ for
$n\ge2$, so $2<t_n\le4$; and
$d_{n+1}-d_n=2^{-n}-\tfrac23\cdot3^{-n}>0$, so $d_n$ increases to $1$ and
$t_n$ decreases to $2$.  Finally $a_n-t_n=2-t_n2^{-n}\ge2-4\cdot2^{-n}>0$ for
$n\ge2$, so the nonleading magnitudes are $a_n$ once and $t_n$ exactly $n$
times with $a_n>t_n>0$; thus $b_2(F_n)=t_n$.  Theorem~\ref{thm:order} with
$(r_1,r_2)=(2,3)$ and $u=1$ gives $b_2\ge2$ for every monic multiple, and
$t_n\to2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of the rows $k=2,3$ at $(2,3,5)$]
\label{prop:sharp235}
For $P(x)=(x-2)(x-3)(x-5)$,
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_2(PQ)=8,
  \qquad
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_3(PQ)=4,
\]
the values \eqref{eq:order} gives at $u=1$ and $u=2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The construction of Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp23} adapts.  Build the
multiple from the top down, with a leading $1$, a short free prefix, a long run of one repeated
value, and the exempt coefficients at the bottom:
\[
  F_D(x)=x^D-a_Dx^{D-1}+t_D\sum_{j=1}^{D-2}x^j+c_D,
  \qquad
  G_D(x)=x^D-s_D\sum_{j=2}^{D-1}x^j+q_Dx+q_D',
\]
with the coefficients determined by vanishing at $2,3,5$.  Dividing
$F_D(r)=0$ by $r^{D-1}$ and writing $\gamma_D=c_D2^{1-D}$ gives
\[
  r-a_D+t_D\frac{1-r^{2-D}}{r-1}+\gamma_D\Bigl(\frac2r\Bigr)^{D-1}=0,
  \qquad r\in\{2,3,5\},
\]
a linear system in $(a_D,t_D,\gamma_D)$ whose coefficients converge, as
$D\to\infty$, to those of $2-a+t+\gamma=0$, $3-a+t/2=0$, $5-a+t/4=0$.  That
limit system has determinant $1/4$ and solution $(a,t,\gamma)=(7,8,-3)$, so
$(a_D,t_D,\gamma_D)\to(7,8,-3)$.  Hence $|c_D|\sim3\cdot2^{D-1}$ and
$a_D<t_D$ for large $D$, and the nonleading magnitudes $a_D$, $t_D$ ($D-2$
times) and $|c_D|$ give $b_2(F_D)=t_D\to8$.  Likewise, with
$(\hat q_D,\hat q_D')=3^{-D}(q_D,q_D')$, dividing $G_D(r)=0$ by $r^D$ for $r=3,5$ and by
$3^D$ for $r=2$ gives
\begin{gather*}
  1-s_D\frac{1-r^{2-D}}{r-1}+(r\hat q_D+\hat q_D')\Bigl(\frac3r\Bigr)^D=0
  \qquad(r=3,5),\\
  \Bigl(\frac23\Bigr)^D\bigl(1-s_D(1-2^{2-D})\bigr)+2\hat q_D+\hat q_D'=0,
\end{gather*}
with limit system $1-s/4=0$, $1-s/2+3\hat q+\hat q'=0$, $2\hat q+\hat q'=0$,
of determinant $-1/4$ and solution $(s,\hat q,\hat q')=(4,1,-2)$.  So $s_D\to4$ while $|q_D|\sim3^D$
and $|q_D'|\sim2\cdot3^D$, and $b_3(G_D)=s_D\to4$.  Theorem~\ref{thm:order} gives $b_2\ge8$ and
$b_3\ge4$ for every monic multiple, so both are infima.
\end{proof}

They are not infima at every root set.  The next proposition computes one
row exactly and finds it strictly above \eqref{eq:order}; the certificate
machinery itself supplies both the improved lower bound and the matching
construction.

\begin{proposition}[Non-sharpness of the row $k=2$ at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$]
\label{prop:nonsharp}
Let $P(x)=(x-\tfrac52)(x-3)(x-\tfrac72)$.  Then
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_2(PQ)=\frac{65}{12},
\]
whereas \eqref{eq:order} gives only $b_2\ge(3-1)(\tfrac72-1)=5$.  The
infimum is not attained.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The nodes are $\rho=(\tfrac72,3,\tfrac52)$ and
$y=(\tfrac27,\tfrac13,\tfrac25)$, with $c=3$ and $y_c=\tfrac25\le\tfrac12$.

\emph{Lower bound.}  This is the optimization described at the start of this subsection: the excluded
set $U$ is a single position, and we place the one free zero differently
according to where that position sits, then take the worst case.  Let $F$ be
a monic multiple of $P$ of degree $D$ and let
$j<D$ be a position with $|f_j|=b_1(F)$; put $z=D-j\ge1$.  For any
certificate $w$ on these nodes with $w_0=1$ and $w_z=0$, the identity in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}, applied with $U=\{j\}$, gives
\[
  1\le\sum_{j'<D,\ j'\ne j}|f_{j'}|\,|w_{D-j'}|\le b_2(F)\Tail(w),
\]
since every position other than $j$ has magnitude at most $b_2(F)$.

If $z\le2$, take $Z=\{1,2\}$.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive},
$\Tail(w)=\prod_iy_i/(1-y_i)=\tfrac25\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac2{15}$,
so $b_2(F)\ge\tfrac{15}2$.

If $z\ge3$, take $Z=\{2,z\}$, so that $Z'=Z\setminus\{z\}=\{2\}$.  The
deletion step in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} gives
$\Tail(w)<\Tail(v)$, where $v$ is the sum on the two nodes
$\tfrac27,\tfrac13$ with $v_0=1$ and $v_2=0$, namely
\[
  v_d=\frac{49}{13}\Bigl(\frac27\Bigr)^{d}-\frac{36}{13}\Bigl(\frac13\Bigr)^{d}.
\]
Being a two-node sum, $v$ has at most one real zero, so $d=2$ is its only
one and $v$ changes sign there: $v_1=\tfrac2{13}>0$ and $v_d<0$ for $d\ge3$.
Since
$\sum_{d\ge1}v_d=\tfrac{49}{13}\cdot\tfrac25-\tfrac{36}{13}\cdot\tfrac12
=\tfrac8{65}$, and $v_2=0$,
\[
  \Tail(v)=2v_1-\sum_{d\ge1}v_d=\frac4{13}-\frac8{65}=\frac{12}{65},
\]
whence $b_2(F)>\tfrac{65}{12}$.  As $\tfrac{65}{12}<\tfrac{15}2$, every monic
multiple has $b_2(F)>\tfrac{65}{12}$, and the infimum is not attained.

\emph{Matching construction.}  For $D\ge6$ let
\[
  F_D(x)=x^D-h_Dx^{D-1}+a_Dx^{D-2}+h_D\sum_{j=1}^{D-3}x^j+c_D,
\]
with $(h_D,a_D,c_D)$ determined by $F_D(\tfrac52)=F_D(3)=F_D(\tfrac72)=0$.
Dividing each equation by $r^D$ and writing $\gamma_D=c_D(\tfrac25)^D$, the
coefficient of $h_D$ contributes
$-r^{-1}+\bigl(r^{-2}-r^{1-D}\bigr)/(r-1)$ and $\gamma_D$ enters only through
$\bigl(5/(2r)\bigr)^D$, which tends to $1$ at $r=\tfrac52$ and to $0$ at
$r=3,\tfrac72$.  The limiting system is therefore
\[
  1-\frac{2h}5+\frac{4a}{25}+\frac{8h}{75}+\gamma=0,\qquad
  1-\frac h3+\frac a9+\frac h{18}=0,\qquad
  1-\frac{2h}7+\frac{4a}{49}+\frac{8h}{245}=0 .
\]
The last two give $2a=5h-18$ and $245-62h+20a=0$, hence $65-12h=0$; solving,
\[
  (h,a,\gamma)=\Bigl(\frac{65}{12},\ \frac{109}{24},\ -\frac{31}{225}\Bigr),
\]
and the system is nonsingular, so $(h_D,a_D,\gamma_D)$ converges to it.  Since
$\gamma\ne0$ we have $|c_D|\sim\tfrac{31}{225}(\tfrac52)^D\to\infty$, and
$a\to\tfrac{109}{24}<\tfrac{65}{12}$.  So for all large $D$ the nonleading
magnitudes are $|c_D|$ once, $h_D$ exactly $D-2$ times and $a_D<h_D$ once,
with $|c_D|>h_D$; hence $b_2(F_D)=h_D\to\tfrac{65}{12}$.
\end{proof}

So sharpness of the rows $k\ge2$ depends on the root set: it holds at
$(2,3)$ and $(2,3,5)$ and fails at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$.  Characterizing
the root sets at which \eqref{eq:order} is an infimum is open.
