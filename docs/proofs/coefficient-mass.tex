\begin{abstract}
Fix real roots $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed.  For every
nonzero real polynomial $F=\sum_jf_jx^j$ of degree $D$ divisible by
$\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$, the $k$-th largest nonleading coefficient
magnitude is at least $|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$ for $1\le k\le L$,
uniformly in the degree.  Summing these rows bounds the logarithmic
coefficient mass $\Lambda(F)=\sum_j\log^+|f_j|$ of a monic multiple below by
$\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$, sharp in order at the first $L$ primes but degenerate
as the roots approach $2$; a Jensen-formula bound of order $L^2$ repairs
this, giving $\inf\Lambda\asymp L^2+\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$ over monic
multiples at every root set with $L\ge2$.  The rows $k\ge2$ are
sharp at $(2,3)$ and $(2,3,5)$, and not sharp at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$,
where the $k=2$ infimum is $65/12$ against $5$.  The key tool compares the
$\ell^1$ tails of normalized exponential sums with prescribed
positive-integer zeros: deleting the largest zero together with the largest
node strictly increases the tail, and iterating gives a product bound over
the initial run of consecutive zeros, with equality only when all the zeros
are consecutive.  As a by-product, the same-sign hypothesis under which
Dubickas and Jankauskas evaluate the consecutive case holds at all distinct
positive nodes, making their bound $H(P)\ge|P(1)|$ unconditional for
distinct real roots greater than one.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Polynomial multiples; coefficient mass; reduced
height; Chebyshev systems; exponential sums.

\section{Introduction}

For a nonzero $F(x)=\sum_jf_jx^j$ of degree $D$, define its logarithmic
coefficient mass by
\[
  \Lambda(F)=\sum_{j:f_j\ne0}\log^+|f_j|,
  \qquad \log^+x=\max(0,\log x),
\]
the logarithm of the multiplicative height $\prod_{j:f_j\ne0}\max(1,|f_j|)$.
The height $\max_j|f_j|$ sees only one coefficient, and the length
$\sum_j|f_j|$ can be spread over many comparable terms, so neither norm
forces many coefficients to be large at once.  The mass does: it is additive
over coefficients, so lower bounds on several coefficient order statistics
add up to a lower bound for $\Lambda$ that no bound on a single height can
give.  For integer coefficients, $\Lambda(F)/\log B$ is roughly the total
number of base-$B$ digits of the nonzero coefficients.  Since $\log^+$
ignores coefficients of magnitude at most one, $\Lambda(\varepsilon F)\to0$
as $\varepsilon\to0$, so every bound below is stated relative to the
leading coefficient $f_D$; the case of interest is the monic one, or more
generally $|f_D|\ge1$, which is also the normalization of the reduced
height~\cite{dj} and costs nothing for integer multiples.

\paragraph{Main results.}
Let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ be real, repetitions allowed, let
$P=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$, and let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of $P$.
Write $b_k(F)$ for the $k$-th largest of the nonleading coefficient
magnitudes $|f_j|$, $j<D$.  Theorem~\ref{thm:order} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} give
\[
  b_k(F)\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^{L}(r_i-1)
  \quad(1\le k\le L),
  \qquad
  \Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1).
\]
We call the first inequality at a given $k$ the row $k$.  Both bounds are
uniform in the degree and in any further roots of the multiple, and they
extend to complex multiples (Corollary~\ref{cor:complex}).  The row $k=1$
holds for all $r_1>1$.  For the first $L$ primes the mass bound is
$\Lambda(F)\ge(\frac12+o(1))L^2\log L$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:prime}), and the
infimum over monic multiples is
$\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum}).
For every root set the product $P$ itself comes within $O(L^2)$ of the
infimum of $\Lambda$ over monic multiples (Corollary~\ref{cor:near}).  As
the roots approach $2$ the mass bound degenerates; a separate argument by
Jensen's formula gives $\Lambda(F)\ge L^2/80$ for monic multiples when
$L\ge8$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic}), and together
\[
  \inf_{Q\text{ monic}}\Lambda(PQ)\ \asymp\ L^2+\sum_{i=1}^Li\log(r_i-1)
  \qquad(L\ge2),
\]
with absolute constants (Corollary~\ref{cor:allroots}).

For comparison, the standard route through the Mahler measure
$\mathcal M$ gives much less.  A monic multiple $F$ of $P$ has
$\mathcal M(F)\ge\prod_ir_i$, and Landau's inequality
$\mathcal M(F)\le\lVert F\rVert_2\le\sqrt{D+1}\,H(F)$ gives
\[
  \Lambda(F)\ \ge\ \log H(F)\ \ge\ \sum_{i=1}^L\log r_i-\tfrac12\log(D+1),
\]
which at the first $L$ primes is of order $L\log L$: it charges one
coefficient, and the number of prescribed roots enters once.
Corollary~\ref{cor:prime} is larger by a factor of order $L$ and has no
degree term, because each of the $L$ roots is charged against a coefficient
of its own.

\paragraph{Method.}
To bound $b_k$, exclude any $k-1$ coefficient positions and build a
normalized exponential sum in the reciprocal roots, the certificate, that
vanishes at those positions and at enough initial ones.  A bound on the
$\ell^1$ tail of this sum then forces some coefficient outside the excluded
set to be large (Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}).  All the difficulty lies in the
tail bound, Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}: it bounds the tail of a certificate by a
product over the initial run of consecutive zeros of its zero set, with
equality only when all the zeros are consecutive.  Its engine is a strict
deletion step: if the largest prescribed zero is displaced, that is, not part
of that run, deleting it together with the largest reciprocal node strictly
increases the tail, even though moving a zero at fixed nodes can push the
tail either way.  Because the resulting bound is uniform in the zero set, it
applies to every choice of excluded positions, and this is what turns
certificates, which earlier controlled only a single height, into bounds on
all the order statistics $b_1,\ldots,b_L$.

\paragraph{Relation to earlier work.}
Reduced length and reduced height minimize the two classical coefficient
norms over normalized multiples (monic ones for the reduced height; for the
reduced length, those with leading or constant coefficient
one)~\cite{dubickas,schinzel,schinzel-ii,schinzel-complex,dj}.  The Euclidean
analogue is effectively computable~\cite{frw}.  Dual problems---an integer or
bounded-height multiple, or a root of prescribed multiplicity for a
restricted-coefficient power series---form a classical
line~\cite{mignotte,bombieri-vaaler,bbbp-multiple,bbbp-ray}, and work on
sparse multiples looks for multiples of prescribed degree, sparsity and
height~\cite{grt}.

Closest to this paper, Dubickas and Jankauskas associate to reciprocal roots
and a prescribed zero set $J$ a normalized determinant sequence $S_n(J)$
with $S_0(J)=1$ and $S_k(J)=0$ for $k\in J$~\cite{dj}.  This sequence is the
exponential sum $w$ studied below, the reciprocal of its infinite tail is
their dual lower bound for the reduced height, their inequality~(8), and
that inequality already allows a prescribed set of positions to be excluded.
They evaluate the consecutive case $J=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$ explicitly, on the
condition that all $S_n$ with $n$ large have the same sign: their
$H(P)\ge|P(1)|$ holds ``provided that all $S_n$ \ldots have the same
sign''~\cite[p.~333]{dj}.  That condition stays a hypothesis through their
Corollary~7~\cite[Section~2]{dj} and is checked only case by
case~\cite[Sections~3--4]{dj}.  Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive} proves it for all
distinct positive real nodes by reading the sign off the complete homogeneous
symmetric polynomials $h_j$, so their bound $H(P)\ge|P(1)|$, the row $k=1$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:order}, holds unconditionally for distinct real roots
greater than one.  What is new here is the tail estimate for arbitrary
integer zero sets, and with it the rows $k\ge2$.

\paragraph{Sharpness.}
Section~\ref{sec:sharp} asks how close the rows are to optimal.  The loss
in the row $k=1$ is unbounded: at suitable distinct
roots a certificate bound for $b_1$ exceeds $|P(1)|$ by a factor of order
$L$, and every monic multiple has $b_1\ge(r_1+\cdots+r_L)/2$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}).  At $r_i=2$, where the mass bound
degenerates, Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} forces two large coefficients.
The rows $k\ge2$ are infima at $(2,3)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp23}) and
$(2,3,5)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp235}), so they cannot be improved by
any constant factor in general, but not at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$, where the
$k=2$ infimum is $65/12$ against the value $5$ of the row
(Proposition~\ref{prop:nonsharp}).  The companion
paper~\cite{coefficient-mass-attainment} characterizes the root sets at which
a row is an infimum by a partial-sum criterion, repeated roots included.
Finally, every monic multiple of $(x-2)^L$ has $b_k\ge L-k+1$ for every
row $1\le k\le L$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow}).

\paragraph{Conventions.}
All logarithms are natural, $\N_{>0}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and every asymptotic
statement is for $L\to\infty$ with absolute implied constants unless
parameters are named.

\section{A displaced-zero tail theorem}

Let $\rho_1>\cdots>\rho_c\ge2$ and $y_i=\rho_i^{-1}$, so that
$0<y_1<y_2<\cdots<y_c\le1/2$.  For any $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ of size $c-1$, the
generalized Vandermonde system
\[
  w_d=\sum_{i=1}^c a_i y_i^d,
  \qquad w_0=1,
  \qquad w_z=0\quad(z\in Z),
\]
determines the coefficients $a_i$ uniquely.  Indeed, a kernel vector would be
a nonzero $c$-node exponential sum with the $c$ zeros $\{0\}\cup Z$,
contradicting the zero bound below.  It is convenient to regard the sequence
$w_0,w_1,\ldots$ as the restriction to $d\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ of the
real-analytic function $d\mapsto\sum_ia_iy_i^d$; all statements about zeros
and signs of an exponential sum refer to this function of the real variable.

\paragraph{Zero bound.}
A nonzero exponential sum on $r$ distinct positive nodes has at most $r-1$
real zeros, counted with multiplicity: write it as $\sum_ia_ie^{\lambda_id}$
with distinct $\lambda_i$, factor out one exponential, and induct on $r$ by
Rolle's theorem, the derivative being a sum on one fewer
node~\cite[Part~Five, Ch.~1]{polya-szego}; equivalently, the $e^{\lambda d}$
form a Chebyshev system~\cite{karlin}.
Consequently a nonzero $r$-node sum with $r-1$
distinct prescribed real zeros has exactly those zeros, each is simple, and the sum
changes sign at each of them.

\begin{lemma}[Consecutive zero set]\label{lem:consecutive}
Let $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c<1$; no cutoff $y_c\le1/2$ is imposed.  Let $w$ be the
exponential sum on these nodes with $w_0=1$ and $w_z=0$ for $z\in\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$;
it exists and is unique by the Vandermonde argument above, which needs only
distinct positive nodes.  Then
\[
  w_d=(-1)^{c+1}\Bigl(\prod_iy_i\Bigr)h_{d-c}(y_1,\ldots,y_c)
  \qquad(d\ge c),
\]
where $h_j$ is the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial, so these terms
are one-signed and
\[
  \Tail(w):=\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|=\prod_{i=1}^c\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put
\[
  U(x)=\sum_{d\ge0}w_dx^d=\frac{N(x)}{\prod_i(1-y_ix)},
  \qquad \deg N\le c-1.
\]
The first $c-1$ nonconstant coefficients vanish, so $N(x)-\prod_i(1-y_ix)$
is a constant multiple of $x^c$; that constant is fixed by the vanishing
$x^c$ coefficient of $N$ (which has degree at most $c-1$), giving
\[
  N(x)=\prod_i(1-y_ix)+(-1)^{c+1}\Bigl(\prod_i y_i\Bigr)x^c .
\]
Since $1/\prod_i(1-y_ix)=\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y_1,\ldots,y_c)x^j$, the displayed
formula for $w_d$ follows, and summing the one-signed terms gives
\[
  \Tail(w)=\Bigl(\prod_i y_i\Bigr)\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y_1,\ldots,y_c)
          =\prod_i\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Displaced-zero tail bound]\label{thm:tail}
Let $\rho_1>\cdots>\rho_c\ge2$, $y_i=\rho_i^{-1}$, $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ with
$|Z|=c-1$, and let $w$ be the sum determined above.  Put
$\ell=\max\{0\le k\le c-1:\{1,\ldots,k\}\subseteq Z\}$, the leading run of
$Z$; the elements of $Z$ outside $\{1,\ldots,\ell\}$ are its displaced zeros.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
  \Tail(w)=\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|
  \le \prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}
  =\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}(\rho_i-1)}.
\end{equation}
Equality holds exactly when $Z=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$.
\end{theorem}

For a concrete two-node example, take $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(3,2)$.  If the
prescribed zero is consecutive, $Z=\{1\}$, then
\[
 w_d=3^{1-d}-2^{1-d},\qquad \Tail(w)=\frac12,
\]
so equality holds in \eqref{eq:tail}.  Moving the zero to $Z=\{2\}$ gives
\[
 w_d=\frac95\,3^{-d}-\frac45\,2^{-d},\qquad \Tail(w)=\frac3{10}<\frac12.
\]
Here moving the zero lowers the tail, but that does not happen in general.
For $(\rho_1,\rho_2)=(5,3)$, $Z=\{1\}$ gives $\Tail(w)=1/8$ while $Z=\{2\}$
gives $\Tail(w)=9/64>1/8$, so a displaced tail can exceed the consecutive
one.  The theorem therefore does not compare zero sets at fixed nodes.
Instead its proof removes the displaced zeros one at a time, starting with
the largest, each time together with the largest reciprocal node; each
displaced zero adds a sign change relative to a comparison sum, and this
sign change lowers the total tail.

The hard step compares a correction sum at the prescribed zeros (the marked
points of Lemma~\ref{lem:interlace}) with its value just past them.  Marked
points come in two kinds: some force the correction sum to vanish, and
others only fix its sign.  The proof of the lemma is a zero count, in which
the sign-fixing points force extra zeros in each gap between them.

\begin{lemma}[Interlacing of the correction sum]\label{lem:interlace}
Let $c\ge2$ and $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c$, let $Z'\subset\N_{>0}$ satisfy $|Z'|=c-2$, and put
$m=\max(Z'\cup\{0\})$; the points of $\{0\}\cup Z'$ are the marked
points.  Let $\widehat F$ be the exponential sum on
$y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ vanishing on $Z'$, and $G$ the sum on
$y_1,\ldots,y_c$ vanishing on $\{0\}\cup Z'$.  Each is determined by those
conditions up to a scalar and, by the zero bound, has constant sign for
$d>m$; normalize both to be positive there.  Write $E_d=G_d-y_cG_{d-1}$,
$\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat F_{m+1}$, and
\[
  \Delta_d=\sigma\widehat F_d-E_d.
\]
Then $\Delta_d\ge0$ for every $d\ge m+1$.  No upper bound on the nodes is
needed.
\end{lemma}

To illustrate the counting in the proof, take
$c=5$ and $Z'=\{2,4,6\}$, so the marked set is $\{0\}\cup Z'=\{0,2,4,6\}$ and
$m=6$.  No two marked points are adjacent, so none of them forces a zero of
$\Delta$.  Instead $\Delta$ is nonzero at $2,4,6$, with signs $-1,+1,-1$.
So each of the gaps $(2,4)$ and $(4,6)$ contains an odd number of zeros of
the $4$-node sum $\Delta$, hence at least one.  Together with the zero at
$m+1=7$ this uses up the whole budget of $c-2=3$ zeros, so there are none
above $7$, and $\Delta$ is positive there.  In tabular form, writing
$\bullet$ for a marked point and listing the sign of $\Delta$ at it:
\[
  \begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
    d & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7\\\hline
    \text{marked} & \bullet & & \bullet & & \bullet & & \bullet & \\
    \sgn\Delta_d  &         & & -       & & +       & & -       & 0\\
    \text{forced zeros in gap} & & & & 0 & & 0 & &
  \end{array}
\]
The two sign changes, across $(2,4)$ and $(4,6)$, each cost a zero; with the
zero at $7$ that is three, the whole budget.  If instead $Z'=\{2,4,5,7\}$
($c=6$), the adjacent pair $\{4,5\}$ forces a zero of $\Delta$ at $5$, while
$\Delta$ is negative at both $4$ and $7$.  Since the signs at $4$ and $7$
agree, $(4,7)$ must contain an even number of zeros, so the forced zero at
$5$ comes with at least one more unit of multiplicity, possibly at $5$
itself.

\begin{proof}
The sums $E$ and $\widehat F$ both live on the $c-1$ nodes
$y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$: writing $G_d=\sum_ia_iy_i^d$ gives
\[
  E_d=\sum_{i=1}^{c-1}a_i(y_i-y_c)y_i^{\,d-1}
      =\sum_{i=1}^{c-1}\beta_iy_i^d,
\]
with $\beta_i=a_i(1-y_c/y_i)$, the $i=c$ term having cancelled.  Hence $\Delta$
is an exponential sum on at most $c-1$ nodes (some coefficients may vanish),
so by the zero bound it is either identically zero or has at most $c-2$ real
zeros counted with multiplicity.

Relabel $A=\{0\}\cup Z'$ as $\alpha_1=0<\alpha_2<\cdots<\alpha_{c-1}=m$; let $r$ be the
number of consecutive pairs $\{\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1}\}=\{d-1,d\}$ in $A$, and
$J=\{j:\alpha_{j+1}\in Z',\ \alpha_j<\alpha_{j+1}-1\}$, so that $|J|=c-2-r$.

By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple zeros $\{0\}\cup Z'$; on a
component $(\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1})$ its sign is $(-1)^{c-1-j}$ because it is positive
past the last zero and flips at each.  We record the zeros of $\Delta$.

First, $\Delta_{m+1}=0$, because $G_m=0$ and $\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat
F_{m+1}$.  Second, if $\alpha_j=\alpha_{j+1}-1$ then $\alpha_j,\alpha_{j+1}\in A$, so
$G_{\alpha_j}=G_{\alpha_{j+1}}=0$, and $\alpha_{j+1}\in Z'$, so $\widehat F_{\alpha_{j+1}}=0$;
thus $\Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-E_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-G_{\alpha_{j+1}}+y_cG_{\alpha_{j+1}-1}=0$.  There
are $r$ such forced zeros, one for each consecutive pair.  At every other
marked point $\alpha_{j+1}\in Z'$ one has $\alpha_j<\alpha_{j+1}-1$, so
$\alpha_{j+1}-1\notin A$ and $G_{\alpha_{j+1}-1}\ne0$, and
\[
  \Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=-E_{\alpha_{j+1}}=y_cG_{\alpha_{j+1}-1},
  \qquad \sgn\Delta_{\alpha_{j+1}}=(-1)^{c-1-j}.
\]

If $J=\varnothing$ then $r=c-2$, and the forced zeros together with
$m+1$ give $r+1=c-1>c-2$ zeros, so $\Delta\equiv0$ and the claim is
immediate.  Assume $J\ne\varnothing$ and order $J=\{j_1<\cdots<j_p\}$, so
$p=|J|=c-2-r$.  If $\Delta\equiv0$ the conclusion again holds, so assume
$\Delta\not\equiv0$; the zero bound then caps its zeros at $c-2$.  Let
$N_\Delta$ be the number of zeros of $\Delta$ counted with multiplicity.

Take consecutive $j<j'$ in $J$.  Strictly between
$\alpha_{j+1}$ and $\alpha_{j'+1}$ lie the $q=j'-j-1$ marked points
$\alpha_{j+2},\ldots,\alpha_{j'}$; none of $j+1,\ldots,j'-1$ lies in $J$, so
each $\alpha_{i+1}$ with $j<i<j'$ has $\alpha_i=\alpha_{i+1}-1$ adjacent and
carries a forced zero; and the endpoint signs
$(-1)^{c-1-j}$, $(-1)^{c-1-j'}$ differ by $(-1)^{q+1}$.  A real-analytic
function, not identically zero, whose endpoint values are nonzero and differ
in sign by $(-1)^{q+1}$ has an odd or even
number of zeros in the open interval according as $q+1$ is odd or even.  So
the number of zeros in $(\alpha_{j+1},\alpha_{j'+1})$, counted with multiplicity, has
the parity of $q+1$ and is at least $q$; hence it is at least $q+1$, one more
than the $q$ forced zeros.  The $r$ forced zeros lie at marked points, and the
parity supplies one additional unit of multiplicity in each of the $p-1$ gaps
between consecutive members of $J$, possibly at a point already forced.  With
the zero at $m+1$ this gives
\[
  N_\Delta\ge r+(p-1)+1=c-2,
\]
which exhausts the zero-multiplicity budget of the $(c-1)$-node sum $\Delta$; in
particular there are no other zeros.

Every one of the $p-1$ extra multiplicities lies strictly below
$\alpha_{j_p+1}$, the gaps being open intervals, so every
zero of $\Delta$ above $\alpha_{j_p+1}$ belongs to the exhaustion just described
and is simple.  There are $c-1-j_p$ of them: the forced zeros at
$\alpha_{j+1}$ for $j_p<j\le c-2$, and the zero at $m+1$.  Since
$\sgn\Delta_{\alpha_{j_p+1}}=(-1)^{c-1-j_p}$ and there is no zero above
$\alpha_{j_p+1}$ but those $c-1-j_p$ simple ones, crossing them flips the sign to
$(-1)^{2(c-1-j_p)}=+1$, and it stays positive.  Thus $\Delta_d\ge0$ for
$d\ge m+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}]
Induct on the number $c-1-\ell$ of displaced zeros.

\emph{Consecutive base case.}  For $Z=\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$ this is
Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, which is \eqref{eq:tail} with $\ell=c-1$.  The
standing cutoff $y_c\le1/2$ is not used there.

\emph{Displaced step.}  Suppose $\ell<c-1$ and set $z=\max Z$.  Since $Z$ has
$c-1$ elements and is not $\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$, we have $z\ge c\ge \ell+2$.  Put
$Z'=Z\setminus\{z\}$ and $m=\max Z'$, taking $m=0$ if $Z'$ is empty.  Let $F$ be the sum on $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with $F_0=1$ and
$F|_{Z'}=0$, and $G$ the sum on all $c$ nodes with
$G|_{\{0\}\cup Z'}=0$ and $G_z=1$.  Then
\[
  w=F+\lambda G,\qquad \lambda=-F_z,
\]
because both sides satisfy the same $c$ interpolation conditions.  The set
$Z'$ has the same leading run $\ell$ as $Z$, and $\ell+1\le c-1$, so the induction
hypothesis bounds $\Tail(F)$ by the right side of \eqref{eq:tail}.  It
remains to prove $\Tail(w)<\Tail(F)$.

\emph{Sign comparison.}  By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple zeros
$\{0\}\cup Z'$ and $F$ exactly $Z'$; below $z$ the sum $w$ has the same simple
zeros $Z'$ and no others, and at $z$ it has one additional simple zero.  Write
$\eta=\sgn(F_z)$ and $\mu=|F_z|$, so $w=F-\eta\mu G$.  Since $F$ and $G$ flip
at exactly the same points of $Z'$, their relative sign
$\sgn(F_d/G_d)$ is constant on $(0,\infty)\setminus Z'$, and evaluating at
$d=z$, where $G_z=1$, shows it equals $\eta$; thus $F_d=\eta\sgn(G_d)|F_d|$ and
$G_d=\sgn(G_d)|G_d|$ for $d>0$, whence
\[
  w_d=\eta\sgn(G_d)\bigl(|F_d|-\mu|G_d|\bigr),
  \qquad
  |w_d|=\bigl||F_d|-\mu|G_d|\bigr|.
\]
The sign of $|F_d|-\mu|G_d|$ is therefore that of $\eta\sgn(G_d)w_d$.  Passing
a point of $Z'$ flips both $\sgn w$ and $\sgn G$, leaving this sign unchanged,
so it is constant on $(0,z)\setminus Z'$.  It is positive there: the
continuous function $\varphi(d)=|F_d|-\mu|G_d|$ has $\varphi(0)=1$, because
$w_0=F_0=1$ and $G_0=0$, and since $|w_d|=|\varphi(d)|$ for $d>0$ it has no
zero in $(0,\min Z)$.  At $z$, where $G$ does not vanish, only $\sgn w$
flips, so the sign is negative for all $d>z$.  Hence
\[
  |F_d|>\mu|G_d|\quad(1\le d<z,\ d\notin Z'),
  \qquad
  |F_d|<\mu|G_d|\quad(d>z),
\]
with equality exactly at the zeros of $w$: at the points of $Z'$ both sides
vanish, and at $z$ one has $|F_z|=\mu|G_z|$.

For an exponential sum $V$, write
\[
  \Gamma_V(z)=\frac{\sum_{d\ge z}|V_d|}{|V_z|},
  \qquad \Sigma_G=\sum_{1\le d<z}|G_d|.
\]
Splitting $\Tail(F)-\Tail(w)$ at $z$ and using the two displayed
inequalities, $|F_z|=\mu|G_z|$ and $|G_z|=1$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split}
  \Tail(F)-\Tail(w)
  =\mu\bigl(2\Gamma_F(z)-\Gamma_G(z)+\Sigma_G\bigr).
\end{equation}

\emph{The correction ratio.}  We claim
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
  \Gamma_G(z)\le\frac{\Gamma_F(z)}{1-y_c}\le2\Gamma_F(z).
\end{equation}
Let $\widehat F_d=\eta F_d>0$ for $d>m$ and
$E_d=G_d-y_cG_{d-1}$, an exponential sum on the first $c-1$ nodes.  We first
show
\begin{equation}\label{eq:drive}
  \frac{E_d}{\widehat F_d}\le
  \frac{G_z}{\widehat F_z}\qquad(d\ge z>m).
\end{equation}

Set $A=\{0\}\cup Z'=\{\alpha_1=0<\cdots<\alpha_{c-1}=m\}$,
$\sigma=G_{m+1}/\widehat F_{m+1}$, and
$\Delta_d=\sigma\widehat F_d-E_d$.  Both $\widehat F$ and $G$ are positive for
$d>m$, the latter because $G_z=1$ with $z>m$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:interlace}
applies and gives $\Delta_d\ge0$ for $d\ge m+1$, that is,
$E_d/\widehat F_d\le\sigma$ for $d\ge m+1$.  Next, writing $G_d=\sum_ia_iy_i^d$, the coefficient $a_c$ of the largest
node is positive: $G_d>0$ for $d>m$ forces $a_c\ge0$, and $a_c=0$ would make
$G$ a sum on the $c-1$ nodes $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with the $c-1$ zeros
$\{0\}\cup Z'$, hence identically zero.  Consider the $c$-node sum
\[
  \Psi_d=\widehat F_{m+1}G_d-G_{m+1}\widehat F_d .
\]
Its $y_c$-coefficient is $\widehat F_{m+1}a_c>0$, so it is not identically
zero and $\Psi_d\sim\widehat F_{m+1}a_cy_c^{\,d}>0$ as $d\to\infty$; it
vanishes on $Z'\cup\{m+1\}$, which is $c-1$ points, so by the zero bound
those are its only zeros and $\Psi$ is positive beyond $m+1$.  Hence
$G_d/\widehat F_d>\sigma$ for $d>m+1$, while $G_z/\widehat F_z=\sigma$ when
$z=m+1$; in either case $\sigma\le G_z/\widehat F_z$.  Therefore
\[
  \frac{E_d}{\widehat F_d}\le\sigma\le\frac{G_z}{\widehat F_z}
  \qquad(d\ge z>m),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:drive}.

Induction in $G_d=y_cG_{d-1}+E_d$, with $G_z=1$ and
\eqref{eq:drive}, gives for $t\ge0$
\[
  \frac{G_{z+t}}{G_z}
  \le\sum_{j=0}^t y_c^{t-j}
       \frac{\widehat F_{z+j}}{\widehat F_z}.
\]
Summing over $t$ (a geometric series in $y_c$) proves the first inequality
in \eqref{eq:gamma}; the second uses $y_c\le1/2$.

\emph{Strictness.}  It remains to see that $\Sigma_G>0$, so that
\eqref{eq:split} is positive.  By the zero bound, $G$ has exactly the simple
zeros $\{0\}\cup Z'$ and none other.  If $z>m+1$, then $m+1<z$ and
$m+1\notin\{0\}\cup Z'$, so $G_{m+1}\ne0$ and $\Sigma_G>0$.  If $z=m+1$, then
$|Z'|=c-2<z-1$ because $z\ge c$, so some $d$ with $1\le d<z$ avoids
$\{0\}\cup Z'$, and again $\Sigma_G>0$.  So the
difference in \eqref{eq:split} is strictly positive, and the one-step
comparison
\[
  \Tail(w)<\Tail(F)
\]
holds.  This is the deletion step, and it is strict: the tail of the $c$-node
sum $w$ is smaller than that of the $(c-1)$-node sum $F$, which keeps the
lower zeros $Z'$ but drops the largest prescribed zero and the largest node.
This completes the induction, and equality in \eqref{eq:tail} can occur only
in the base case.
\end{proof}

The restriction $\rho_i\ge2$ matters.  For $c=2$,
$y_1=1/10$, $y_2=4/5$, and $Z=\{2\}$, the normalized coefficients are
$a_1=64/63$ and $a_2=-1/63$, so
$\Tail(w)=52/405>1/9=y_1/(1-y_1)$: \eqref{eq:tail} fails once a node
passes the cutoff.  The cutoff enters the argument at two places only:
$1/(1-y_c)\le2$ in \eqref{eq:gamma} here, and the factors discarded in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} below.  It cannot be relaxed: the companion
paper~\cite{coefficient-mass-attainment} shows that for every $a>1/2$ some
nodes in $(0,a]$ violate \eqref{eq:tail}.

\section{Coefficient order statistics}

Let
\[
 P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i),\qquad 2\le r_1<\cdots<r_L,
\]
with the roots distinct for the lemma below (repetitions are handled in
Theorem~\ref{thm:order}), and let $F(x)=\sum_{j=0}^Df_jx^j\in\R[x]$ be a
nonzero multiple of $P$.  As in the introduction, $b_1\ge b_2\ge\cdots$ are
the magnitudes $|f_j|$, $j<D$, in decreasing order.

\begin{lemma}[Certificate with excluded positions]\label{lem:slack}
Assume the roots $2\le r_1<\cdots<r_L$ are distinct and $|f_D|=1$.  Fix $0\le u\le L-1$, with $r_1>1$
in place of $r_1\ge2$ permitted when $u=0$.  For every set $U$ of $u$
coefficient positions below $D$, some $j<D$ outside $U$ satisfies
\[
 |f_j|\ge\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use all $c=L$ roots $\rho_1=r_L>\rho_2=r_{L-1}>\cdots>\rho_c=r_1$.  A
position $j$ has backward distance $d=D-j$.  Let $W\subseteq\N_{>0}$ be the
set of distances of the positions in $U$, and let $w$ be the certificate on
these $c$ nodes, with $w_0=1$ and with zero set $Z$ consisting of $W$
together with the first $L-u-1$ positive distances outside $W$.  The $u$
positions have distinct distances, so $|W|=u$, and $L-u-1\ge0$ because
$u\le L-1$; hence $|Z|=c-1$, as required; write $w_d=\sum_i a_iy_i^d$.

The dual-certificate identity is explicit.  Since $F(\rho_i)=0$ for the $c$
chosen roots,
\[
  0=\sum_{i=1}^c a_i\rho_i^{-D}F(\rho_i)
   =\sum_{j\le D}f_jw_{D-j}
   =f_D+\sum_{j<D}f_jw_{D-j},
\]
using $w_0=1$.  The terms with $j\in U$ vanish because $D-j\in W\subseteq Z$.
Hence, as $|f_D|=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual}
  1\le\sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|f_j|\,|w_{D-j}|
  \le\Bigl(\max_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|f_j|\Bigr)
        \sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|w_{D-j}|.
\end{equation}

The set $Z$ contains $\{1,\ldots,L-u-1\}$: a distance $t\le L-u-1$ is in
$W\subseteq Z$ if occupied by $U$, and otherwise is among the first
$L-u-1$ distances outside $W$.  Thus the leading run $\ell$ of $Z$ is at least
$L-u-1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} bounds the tail by the product of
$y_i/(1-y_i)$ over the $\ell+1\ge L-u$ smallest reciprocal nodes $y_1,\ldots,y_{\ell+1}$.  Every
such factor is at most $1$, since $y_i\le1/2$ is exactly $\rho_i\ge2$, so
discarding all but the first $L-u$ factors only weakens the bound: the tail is
at most the product over the $L-u$ smallest reciprocal nodes.  These are
$\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_{L-u}$, that is,
$r_L,r_{L-1},\ldots,r_{u+1}$; the last index is
$L-(L-u)+1=u+1$, which is why the product below starts there.  For $u=0$
nothing is discarded: there $Z=\{1,\ldots,L-1\}$, so $\Tail(w)$ is evaluated
exactly by Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, which needs only $y_i<1$, and the
bound holds already for $r_1>1$.  Therefore
\[
  \sum_{j<D,\ j\notin U}|w_{D-j}|
  \le\sum_{d\ge1}|w_d|=\Tail(w)
  \le\prod_{i=1}^{L-u}\frac{\rho_i^{-1}}{1-\rho_i^{-1}}
  =\frac{1}{\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)}.
\]
Substituting into \eqref{eq:dual} gives
$1\le\bigl(\max_{j<D,j\notin U}|f_j|\bigr)/\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)$, which is
the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Coefficient order statistics]\label{thm:order}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ be real with $r_1\ge2$, repetitions allowed, and
let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.  Then for every
$0\le u\le L-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
  b_{u+1}\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1).
\end{equation}
If instead only $r_1>1$ is assumed, \eqref{eq:order} still holds for $u=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume first that the $r_i$ are distinct and $|f_D|=1$.  The assertion that
some $j<D$ outside an arbitrary set $U$ of $u$ positions has $|f_j|\ge T$ is
equivalent to the assertion that at least $u+1$ positions $j<D$ have
$|f_j|\ge T$, that is, to $b_{u+1}\ge T$: any $U$ of size $u$ misses one of
the $u+1$ large positions, and conversely the $u$ largest positions form a set
$U$ that the $(u+1)$-st largest avoids.  Apply this with
$T=\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)$ to the conclusion of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}.

For general $f_D\ne0$, the quotient $F/f_D$ has leading coefficient $1$ and
coefficient magnitudes $b_k(F)/|f_D|$, so the normalized case gives
$b_k(F)\ge|f_D|\,T$ for every $k$.

For repetitions, put $r_{i,\varepsilon}=r_i+i\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon>0$, so that $2\le r_{1,\varepsilon}<\cdots<r_{L,\varepsilon}$ and
$r_{i,\varepsilon}\to r_i$, and write $F=Q\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ and
$F_\varepsilon=Q\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_{i,\varepsilon})$ with the same $Q$.  Then
$\deg F_\varepsilon=D$, the leading coefficient is still $f_D$, and each
coefficient of $F_\varepsilon$ is a polynomial in $\varepsilon$, so the
coefficient vector of $F_\varepsilon$ tends to that of $F$; an order statistic of a converging tuple is continuous, so
$b_{u+1}(F_\varepsilon)\to b_{u+1}(F)$.  The distinct case gives
$b_{u+1}(F_\varepsilon)\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_{i,\varepsilon}-1)$, and letting
$\varepsilon\to0$ proves \eqref{eq:order}.

The $u=0$ clause follows from the $u=0$ clause of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} by
the same perturbation, which keeps $r_{i,\varepsilon}>1$.
\end{proof}

With $k=u+1$, \eqref{eq:order} reads
$b_k\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$ for $1\le k\le L$, the form used in the
introduction and in Corollary~\ref{cor:complex}.

\begin{corollary}[Complex multiples]\label{cor:complex}
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} holds verbatim for a nonzero $F\in\mathbb C[x]$
divisible by $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $g=F/f_D=PQ$ with $Q\in\mathbb C[x]$ monic.  Since $P$ is real,
$\operatorname{Re}g=P\operatorname{Re}Q$ is a monic real multiple of $P$ of
the same degree $D$, and $|\operatorname{Re}g_j|\le|g_j|$ for every $j$.
Coefficientwise domination passes to order statistics, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} applied to $\operatorname{Re}g$ gives
$b_k(g)\ge b_k(\operatorname{Re}g)\ge\prod_{i=k}^L(r_i-1)$; multiplying
through by $|f_D|$ gives \eqref{eq:order} for $F$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Logarithmic mass]\label{cor:mass}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ be real with $r_1\ge2$, repetitions allowed, and
let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass}
 \Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each product in \eqref{eq:order} is at least one because $r_i\ge2$, so the
$L$ largest nonleading magnitudes are nonzero, as is $f_D$; since $D\ge L$
these are $L+1$ distinct positions.  Summing $\log^+\ge\log$ over them alone,
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\log|f_D|+\sum_{u=0}^{L-1}\log b_{u+1}
 \ge(L+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\log(r_i-1),
\]
using $\log b_{u+1}\ge\log|f_D|+\sum_{i=u+1}^L\log(r_i-1)$ and collecting the
index-$i$ terms, which occur exactly $i$ times.
\end{proof}

At the bottom of the allowed range the two bounds behave differently.  For
$r_1=\cdots=r_L=2$ every product in \eqref{eq:order} equals $1$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:order} still forces $L$ nonleading coefficients of
magnitude at least $|f_D|$, while \eqref{eq:mass} reduces to
$\Lambda(F)\ge(L+1)\log|f_D|$ and says nothing more.  The order-statistic
bound stays informative over the whole root range; the mass bound does not.
Section~\ref{sec:sharp} shows that this degeneration is in the bound and not
in $\Lambda$: Propositions~\ref{prop:unbounded}, \ref{prop:neartwo} and
\ref{prop:quadratic} recover for monic multiples, at $r_i=2$, first
$b_1\ge L$, then
$b_2\ge L-1$, and finally a mass of order $L^2$.

\begin{corollary}[Prime roots]\label{cor:prime}
Let $r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ with $r_i\ge p_i$, the $i$th prime, and let $F$ be
a nonzero real multiple of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ with $|f_D|\ge1$.  Then
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\Bigl(\frac12+o(1)\Bigr)L^2\log L .
\]
In particular $\Lambda(F)=\Omega(L^2\log L)$, and the bound is asymptotically
attained: for $F=P_L=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-p_i)$,
$\Lambda(P_L)\le(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $p_i$ for the $i$th prime, so $r_i\ge p_i\sim i\log i$ and, since
$r_i-1\ge p_i-1$,
$\log(r_i-1)\ge\log p_i-O(1/p_i)$.  Drop the nonnegative term
$(L+1)\log|f_D|$ from \eqref{eq:mass}.  Hence
\[
 \Lambda(F)\ge\sum_{i=1}^L i\log(r_i-1)
 \ge\sum_{i=2}^L i\bigl(\log i+\log\log i+o(1)\bigr),
\]
the $i=1$ term being nonnegative, and
$\sum_{i\le L}i\log i=(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$, so the remaining terms
are $o(L^2\log L)$.  This is $\bigl(\tfrac12+o(1)\bigr)L^2\log L$.

For the matching upper bound, the nonleading coefficients of $P_L$ are the
elementary symmetric sums $e_k(p_1,\ldots,p_L)\le\binom{L}{k}p_L^k$, so
\[
 \Lambda(P_L)\le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom{L}{k}
 +\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L
 \le L^2\log2+\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L,
\]
and $\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L=(\tfrac12+o(1))L^2\log L$ while
$L^2\log2=o(L^2\log L)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sharp prime-root infimum]\label{cor:infimum}
Let $P_L=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-p_i)$ and
\[
  M_L=\inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}\Lambda(QP_L).
\]
Then
\[
  M_L=\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} with $r_i=p_i$ and $|f_D|=1$ gives, for every monic
multiple $F$ of $P_L$,
\[
  \Lambda(F)\ge\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log(p_i-1)
  =\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2),
\]
the $i=1$ term being zero.  The equality holds because
$i\log i<p_i<i(\log i+\log\log i)$ for $i\ge6$~\cite{rosser-schoenfeld}
gives $\log(p_i-1)=\log i+\log\log i+O(1)$ for $i\ge2$, while
$\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log i=\frac12L^2\log L+O(L^2)$ and
$\sum_{2\le i\le L}i\log\log i=\frac12L^2\log\log L+o(L^2)$.  The matching upper
bound is the one for $P_L$ from Corollary~\ref{cor:prime},
\[
  \Lambda(P_L)\le L^2\log2+\frac{L(L+1)}2\log p_L
  =\frac12L^2\log L+\frac12L^2\log\log L+O(L^2).
\]
\end{proof}

Multiplying by another factor can lower the mass, so $P_L$ need not
minimize it, even for small $L$.  For example, $P_2=(x-2)(x-3)=x^2-5x+6$ has
$\Lambda(P_2)=\log30$, while $(x+1)P_2=x^3-4x^2+x+6$ has $\Lambda=\log24$,
since coefficients of magnitude one contribute nothing.
Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum} describes the infimum $M_L$ asymptotically; it
does not say that $P_L$ attains it for any finite $L$.

\begin{corollary}[Near-optimality of the root product]\label{cor:near}
Let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed,
$P=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$, and
\[
  M(P)=\inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}\Lambda(PQ).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:near}
  0\le\Lambda(P)-M(P)
  \le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom Lk
    +\sum_{i=1}^L i\log\frac{r_i}{r_i-1}
  =O(L^2),
\end{equation}
with an absolute implied constant.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Taking $Q=1$ gives $M(P)\le\Lambda(P)$, while Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} gives
$M(P)\ge\sum_{i=1}^L i\log(r_i-1)$, because a monic multiple has $|f_D|=1$.
In the other direction the nonleading coefficients of $P$ are the elementary
symmetric sums, and each $k$-fold product of roots is at most the product of
the $k$ largest, so
\[
  e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)\le\binom Lk\prod_{i=L-k+1}^{L}r_i .
\]
Since $e_k\ge\binom Lk2^k>1$, summing logarithms gives
\[
  \Lambda(P)=\sum_{k=1}^L\log e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)
  \le\sum_{k=1}^L\log\binom Lk+\sum_{i=1}^L i\log r_i ,
\]
the sum over $k$ collecting $\log r_i$ exactly $i$ times.
Subtracting the lower bound and using
$\log r_i-\log(r_i-1)=\log\frac{r_i}{r_i-1}$ gives \eqref{eq:near}; finally
$\binom Lk\le2^L$ and $r_i/(r_i-1)\le2$ because $r_i\ge2$, so the right side
is at most $(L^2+\frac{L(L+1)}2)\log2=O(L^2)$.
\end{proof}

In words, no monic multiplier lowers the mass of the root product by more
than $O(L^2)$.  So whenever $\Lambda(P)$ grows faster than $L^2$, as for the
prime-root product of Corollary~\ref{cor:prime} with
$\Lambda(P_L)\sim\frac12L^2\log L$, the product $P$ is asymptotically optimal
among monic multiples.  For the first $L$ primes, Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum}
gives the sharp form of this statement.

\section{Sharpness}\label{sec:sharp}

How close is Theorem~\ref{thm:order} to optimal?  This section treats the
first row; then the bottom of the root range, where \eqref{eq:mass}
degenerates, together with the $L^2$ term that repairs it at every root set;
then the rows $k\ge2$; and finally, at the roots $r_i=2$, a degree-free
reformulation of the rows that proves the per-row bound
$b_k\ge L-k+1$ stated in Section~\ref{sec:bottom}.

\subsection{The first row}

For a monic multiple, row $k=1$ of \eqref{eq:order} is
$b_1\ge\prod_i(r_i-1)=|P(1)|$.  Already for $L=1$ this bound is approached but
never attained: in $F=(x-r)\sum_{i\le m}c_ix^i$ with $c_m=1$, every $|f_j|\le r-1$
would force $c_{j-1}=rc_j+f_j\ge rc_j-(r-1)$, hence $c_j\ge1$ downward from
$c_m$ and $|f_0|=r|c_0|\ge r$.  It is approached: taking
$c_j=1+\varepsilon-\varepsilon r^{m-j}$ with $\varepsilon=1/(r^m-1)$ gives
$c_0=0$ and $|f_j|=(r-1)(1+\varepsilon)$ for $1\le j\le m$, so $b_1\to r-1$
as $m\to\infty$.

Nor is row $k=1$ the best bound the certificate method gives.  The
consecutive certificate, which Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} uses when $u=0$, need
not have the smallest tail at fixed nodes.  Take $P=(x-2)(x-3)$,
nodes $\rho=(3,2)$ and $Z=\{2\}$, whose tail is $3/10$ by the example after
Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}.  Then \eqref{eq:dual}
with $U=\varnothing$ gives
\[
  b_1\ge10/3
\]
for every monic multiple, compared with $|P(1)|=2$.  Corollary~8
of~\cite{dj}, a closed formula for the reduced height of $(x-a)(x-b)$,
evaluates at $(a,b)=(3,2)$ to $10/3$, so here the row is off by a factor of
$5/3$.

Since $10/3$ is the reduced height, $Z=\{2\}$ is the best zero set at these
nodes, and the certificate method loses nothing at $(2,3)$.  The optimum need
not be displaced---at nodes $(5,3)$ the consecutive set is already
optimal---and at small root sets the gain is modest.  The least tail over
zero sets in a bounded range, computed in exact arithmetic (the minimizers
lie well inside the range), gives the following ratios of the certificate
bound to $|P(1)|$:
\[
  \begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
    \text{roots} & (2,3) & (2,5) & (3,5) & (2,3,5) & (2,3,7) & (3,5,7) & (2,3,5,7)\\\hline
    \text{ratio} & \tfrac53 & \tfrac75 & 1 & \tfrac{31}{17} & \tfrac{41}{23}
      & \tfrac{71}{62} & \tfrac{39799}{21337}
  \end{array}
\]
They stay below $2$, but that is a feature of these small root sets and
not a general phenomenon.

\begin{proposition}[Unbounded looseness of the row $k=1$]
\label{prop:unbounded}
Let $1<r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed, and put
$B=\prod_{i=1}^L(r_i-1)$ and $S=\sum_{i=1}^Lr_i$.  Then every monic multiple
$F$ of $\prod_i(x-r_i)$ satisfies
\[
  b_1(F)\ \ge\ \frac{SB}{B+1},
\]
whose ratio to the value $B=|P(1)|$ given by \eqref{eq:order} at $u=0$ is
$S/(B+1)$.  If moreover $r_i\ge2$ for every $i$, then $B\ge1$ and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:halfsum}
  b_1(F)\ \ge\ \frac S2=\frac{r_1+\cdots+r_L}2 .
\end{equation}
Neither \eqref{eq:halfsum} nor the row $k=1$ of \eqref{eq:order} implies the
other.  For a general nonzero leading coefficient both right sides acquire a
factor $|f_D|$, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  For $r_i=2+iL^{-3}$ the ratio $S/(B+1)$ is $\sim L$, so the ratio
between a certificate bound and $|P(1)|$ is unbounded in $L$, already
for distinct roots.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume first that the roots are distinct.  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$, of size
$L-1$, and let $w$ be the certificate on the
nodes $y_i=r_i^{-1}$, so $w_0=1$ and $w_2=\cdots=w_L=0$.  Write
$W(t)=\sum_{d\ge0}w_dt^d=N(t)/\Pi(t)$ with $\Pi(t)=\prod_i(1-y_it)$ and
$\deg N\le L-1$.  Then $N(t)-(1+w_1t)\Pi(t)$ has degree at most $L+1$ and is
divisible by $t^{L+1}$, hence equals $\kappa t^{L+1}$, so
\[
  W(t)=1+w_1t+\frac{\kappa t^{L+1}}{\Pi(t)} .
\]
The coefficient of $t^L$ in $N$ vanishes, which reads
\[
  (-1)^Le_L(y)+w_1(-1)^{L-1}e_{L-1}(y)=0,
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  e_{L-1}(y)=e_L(y)\sum_{i}y_i^{-1}=e_L(y)\,S,
\]
so that $w_1=1/S$.  Then
$\kappa=-w_1[t^L]\Pi(t)=(-1)^{L+1}e_L(y)/S$, and $\Pi(t)=e_L(y)\prod_i(r_i-t)$,
so the last term is $(-1)^{L+1}t^{L+1}/\bigl(S\prod_i(r_i-t)\bigr)$.
Expanding $1/\Pi(t)=\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)t^j$ gives
$w_{L+1+j}=(-1)^{L+1}e_L(y)h_j(y)/S$, which is one-signed, so
\[
  \Tail(w)=w_1+\frac{e_L(y)}S\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)
  =\frac1S+\frac1S\prod_{i}\frac{y_i}{1-y_i}
  =\frac{1+B^{-1}}S .
\]
The dual-certificate identity in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}, applied
with $U=\varnothing$ and this $w$, gives $b_1(F)\ge1/\Tail(w)=SB/(B+1)$.  The
computation used only $0<y_i<1$, that is $r_i>1$.

For repetitions, perturb as in Theorem~\ref{thm:order}: the roots
$r_{i,\varepsilon}=r_i+i\varepsilon$ are distinct and exceed $1$, the
coefficient vector of $F_\varepsilon$ tends to that of $F$ so
$b_1(F_\varepsilon)\to b_1(F)$, and $B$ and $S$ are continuous in the roots.
Letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives the bound in general.

If every $r_i\ge2$ then each $r_i-1\ge1$, so $B\ge1$; as $B\mapsto B/(B+1)$
increases, $B/(B+1)\ge\tfrac12$ and \eqref{eq:halfsum} follows.  Neither
bound dominates: at $r=(3,5)$ one has $S/2=4<8=B$, while at $r=(2,\ldots,2)$
one has $S/2=L>1=B$.

Finally, for $r_i=2+iL^{-3}$ one has
$\log B=\sum_i\log(1+iL^{-3})=O(L^{-1})$, so $B\to1$, while
$S=2L+O(L^{-1})$; hence $S/(B+1)\sim L$.
\end{proof}

At those roots \eqref{eq:order} gives $b_1\ge B\to1$, which is nearly vacuous,
while the certificate above gives $b_1\gtrsim L$: the product bound collapses
as the roots approach $2$, but the number of roots still forces a large
coefficient.  The zero set $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$ used here is not always
optimal---at $(2,3,5)$ it gives $80/9$, against $248/17$ for
$Z=\{1,3\}$---but it suffices to settle the boundedness question.

\subsection{The bottom of the range}\label{sec:bottom}

At $r_i=2$ the certificate of Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded} gives
$b_1\ge L$, where the row $k=1$ of \eqref{eq:order} gives only $1$.  A
different choice of zero set reaches the second coefficient as well.

\begin{proposition}[Two large coefficients at the bottom of the range]
\label{prop:neartwo}
Let $L\ge2$.  Every monic multiple $F$ of $(x-2)^L$ satisfies
\[
  b_1(F)\ge L,\qquad b_2(F)\ge L-1,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \Lambda(F)\ge\log\bigl(L(L-1)\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the perturbation of Theorem~\ref{thm:order} it suffices to bound $b_1$ and
$b_2$ for distinct roots $2<r_1<\cdots<r_L$ and let every $r_i\to2$.  The
first bound is \eqref{eq:halfsum}, whose right side $S/2$ tends to $L$.

For the second, let $z$ be the backward distance of a position attaining
$b_1(F)$ and apply the identity of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} with $U$ that single
position: any certificate $w$ with $w_z=0$ gives $1\le b_2(F)\Tail(w)$.  Write
$e_k(r)=e_k(r_1,\ldots,r_L)$ and $y_i=r_i^{-1}$, so $e_1(r)=S$,
$e_L(r)=\prod_ir_i$, $e_L(y)=1/e_L(r)$, $e_{L-1}(y)=S/e_L(r)$, $e_{L-2}(y)=e_2(r)/e_L(r)$
and $\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)=\prod_i(1-y_i)^{-1}=e_L(r)/B$.

\emph{Case $z=1$.}  Take $Z=\{1,\ldots,L\}\setminus\{2\}$, so $w_0=1$,
$w_1=w_3=\cdots=w_L=0$ and $w_2$ is free.  As in
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}, $\bigl(W(t)-(1+w_2t^2)\bigr)\Pi(t)$ is
divisible by $t^{L+1}$ and has degree at most $L+2$, so
\[
  W(t)=1+w_2t^2+\frac{(\kappa+\lambda t)\,t^{L+1}}{\Pi(t)} .
\]
Comparing the coefficients of $t^L$, $t^{L+1}$ and $t^{L+2}$ gives
\[
  w_2=-\frac{e_L(y)}{e_{L-2}(y)},\qquad
  \kappa=w_2(-1)^Le_{L-1}(y),\qquad
  \lambda=-w_2(-1)^Le_L(y),
\]
so that $w_d=w_2(-1)^L\bigl(e_{L-1}(y)h_{d-L-1}(y)-e_L(y)h_{d-L-2}(y)\bigr)$
for $d\ge L+1$.  These terms are one-signed: $h_j\ge y_Lh_{j-1}$, since
multiplying each monomial of degree $j-1$ by $y_L$ gives distinct monomials
of degree $j$, while $e_{L-1}(y)=\sum_ie_L(y)/y_i\ge e_L(y)/y_L$.  Summing,
\[
  \Tail(w)=|w_2|\Bigl(1+\Bigl[\sum_{j\ge0}h_j(y)\Bigr]
                       \bigl(e_{L-1}(y)-e_L(y)\bigr)\Bigr)
  =\frac1{e_2(r)}\Bigl(1+\frac{S-1}B\Bigr)
  =\frac{B+S-1}{e_2(r)\,B} .
\]
As every $r_i\to2$ we have $B\to1$, $S\to2L$ and $e_2(r)\to2L(L-1)$, so
$\Tail(w)\to1/(L-1)$.

\emph{Case $2\le z\le L$.}  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L\}$.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}, $\Tail(w)=(1+B^{-1})/S\to1/L$.

\emph{Case $z>L$.}  Take $Z=\{2,\ldots,L-1\}\cup\{z\}$, whose largest element
is $z$.  The deletion step in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} removes $z$
together with the largest reciprocal node, leaving exactly the certificate of
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded} on the $L-1$ largest roots; so
$\Tail(w)<(1+B'^{-1})/S'$, where $B'\to1$ and $S'\to2(L-1)$, and the limit is
again $1/(L-1)$.

The three bounds hold for every $\varepsilon$ and none of the three tail
bounds depends on $z$, so for each $\varepsilon$ the multiple $F_\varepsilon$
satisfies $b_2(F_\varepsilon)\ge1/T_\varepsilon$, where $T_\varepsilon$ is
the largest of the three bounds.  Since $T_\varepsilon\to1/(L-1)$
and $b_2(F_\varepsilon)\to b_2(F)$, letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives
$b_2(F)\ge L-1$.  Finally $b_1$ and $b_2$ are attained at distinct positions
and $L-1\ge1$, so $\Lambda(F)\ge\log L+\log(L-1)$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} recovers two coefficients at $r_i=2$.  Far
more is true, and at every root set, not only at $r_i=2$: the infimum of the mass over monic multiples is of order $L^2$
throughout the range where \eqref{eq:mass} degenerates.  The proof uses
Jensen's formula rather than a certificate.  It is close in spirit to the
work of Beaucoup, Borwein, Boyd and Pinner on how high a zero a power series
with constant term $1$ and bounded coefficients can have at a prescribed
point~\cite{bbbp-multiple,bbbp-ray}; the difference is that $k-1$ coefficients
are exempted here rather than all being bounded.

\begin{proposition}[Quadratic mass at every root set]
\label{prop:quadratic}
Let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, repetitions allowed.  Every monic real
multiple $F$ of $\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ satisfies, for every
$\tfrac12<\rho<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jensen}
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ \frac{(2\rho)^{L-k+1}-1}{(1-\rho)^{-k}-1},\qquad 1\le k\le L,
\end{equation}
and hence $\Lambda(F)\ge L^2/80$ for $L\ge8$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $D=\deg F$ and let $F^*(t)=t^DF(1/t)=\sum_{d=0}^Df_{D-d}t^d$ be the
reciprocal polynomial, so $F^*(0)=f_D=1$, while
$F^*(1/r_i)=r_i^{-D}F(r_i)=0$, so $F^*$ has at least $L$ zeros in $(0,\tfrac12]$ counted with multiplicity,
namely the $1/r_i$.

For a real polynomial $h$ and an integer $z\ge1$ put $T_zh=h-(t/z)h'$, so that
$[t^d](T_zh)=(1-d/z)[t^d]h$: the operator annihilates the coefficient at
$t^z$ and fixes the constant term.  It also satisfies
\[
  T_zh=-\frac{t^{z+1}}z\bigl(t^{-z}h\bigr)' .
\]
If $h$ has $m$ zeros in an interval $I\subset(0,\infty)$, counted with
multiplicity, then so does $t^{-z}h$, and by Rolle's theorem its derivative
has at least $m-1$ zeros in the convex hull of them, hence in $I$; the factor
$-t^{z+1}/z$ contributes no zero in $I$.  So $T_zh$ retains at least $m-1$
zeros in $I$.

Fix $k$ and let $z_1<\cdots<z_{k-1}$ be the backward distances $D-j$ of the
positions $j<D$ carrying the $k-1$ largest nonleading magnitudes.  Put
$g=T_{z_{k-1}}\cdots T_{z_1}F^*$, so that
\[
  [t^d]g=[t^d]F^*\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(1-\frac d{z_i}\Bigr).
\]
Then $g(0)=1$, the coefficients at $d=z_1,\ldots,z_{k-1}$ vanish, and by the
previous paragraph $g$ has at least $L-k+1$ zeros in $(0,\tfrac12]$.

If $d\ge1$ and $d\notin\{z_i\}$ then $|[t^d]F^*|=|f_{D-d}|\le b_k(F)$, that
position not being among the $k-1$ largest.  The $z_i$ are distinct positive
integers, so $z_i\ge i$ and
\[
  \prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl|1-\frac d{z_i}\Bigr|
  \le\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(1+\frac di\Bigr)=\binom{d+k-1}{k-1} .
\]
Hence $|[t^d]g|\le b_k(F)\binom{d+k-1}{k-1}$ for $d\ge1$, and since
$\sum_{d\ge0}\binom{d+k-1}{k-1}x^d=(1-x)^{-k}$, taking $x=\rho$ gives
\[
  \max_{|t|=\rho}|g(t)|\ \le\ 1+b_k(F)\bigl((1-\rho)^{-k}-1\bigr).
\]
Jensen's formula for $g$ on $|t|\le\rho$, with $g(0)=1$ and at least
$L-k+1$ zeros of modulus at most $\tfrac12$, gives
$(2\rho)^{L-k+1}\le\max_{|t|=\rho}|g|$, which is \eqref{eq:jensen}.

Now take $\rho=\tfrac9{10}$, let $L\ge8$ and $1\le k\le\lfloor L/8\rfloor$.  Then
$(9/5)^{L-k+1}-1\ge(9/5)^{7L/8}\bigl(1-(9/5)^{-7L/8}\bigr)$ and
$10^k-1\le10^{L/8}$, so
\[
  \log b_k(F)\ \ge\ \tfrac78L\log\tfrac95-\tfrac18L\log10
    +\log\bigl(1-(9/5)^{-7L/8}\bigr)\ \ge\ \tfrac L5 ,
\]
the numerical values being $\tfrac78\log\tfrac95=0.5143\ldots$ and
$\tfrac18\log10=0.2878\ldots$, and the last term at least $-0.017$ for
$L\ge8$.  These $\lfloor L/8\rfloor$ magnitudes sit at distinct positions and
exceed $1$, and $\lfloor L/8\rfloor\ge L/16$, so
$\Lambda(F)\ge(L/16)(L/5)=L^2/80$.
\end{proof}

Combining this with the certificate bound closes the mass problem to within
absolute constants over the whole root range.

\begin{corollary}[The mass infimum at every root set]\label{cor:allroots}
Let $L\ge2$, let $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$, put $P=\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$ and
$M(P)=\inf\{\Lambda(PQ):Q\in\R[x]\text{ monic}\}$.  Then
\[
  M(P)\ \asymp\ L^2+\sum_{i=1}^Li\log(r_i-1),
\]
with absolute implied constants.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the lower bound, Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic} gives
$M(P)\ge L^2/80$ when $L\ge8$, and \eqref{eq:halfsum} gives
$b_1\ge L\ge2$, hence $M(P)\ge\log2\ge L^2\log2/49$, when $2\le L\le7$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} with $|f_D|=1$ gives
$M(P)\ge\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$, so $M(P)$ is at least a constant multiple of
the sum of the two terms.
For the upper bound take $Q=1$: as in Corollary~\ref{cor:near},
$\Lambda(P)\le\sum_k\log\binom Lk+\sum_ii\log r_i$, and
$\sum_ii\log r_i\le\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)+\tfrac{L(L+1)}2\log2$ because
$r_i/(r_i-1)\le2$, so
$\Lambda(P)\le2L^2\log2+\sum_ii\log(r_i-1)$.
\end{proof}

The two terms take turns.  For the first $L$ primes the sum dominates and
Corollary~\ref{cor:infimum} gives the sharp constant; at $r_i=2$ the sum
vanishes and the $L^2$ term is everything.

Three remarks on \eqref{eq:jensen}.  First, it is useful only while
$(1-\rho)^{-k}$ has not overtaken $(2\rho)^{L-k+1}$, that is for $k$ up to a
constant multiple of $L$; it says nothing about the last rows.  In the first
rows, by contrast, Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} remains the stronger
statement for moderate $L$: row $k=1$ of \eqref{eq:jensen} at
$\rho=\tfrac9{10}$ reads $b_1\ge\bigl((9/5)^L-1\bigr)/9$, which overtakes
the bound $b_1\ge L$ of \eqref{eq:halfsum} only at $L=8$ and is exponential
thereafter.  Second, the constant $1/80$ comes from one radius
and one cutoff $k\le L/8$, chosen for clean arithmetic; a sharper choice
would improve it, and we have not pursued the best value.

Third, for monic multiples of $(x-2)^L$ we prove the per-row
statement
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ L-k+1\qquad(1\le k\le L),
\]
which interpolates between $b_1\ge L$ and the row $b_L\ge1$ of
\eqref{eq:order}.  Several rows are already covered:
Proposition~\ref{prop:neartwo} gives $k=1,2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:order} gives
$k=L$, and for $k\le L/8$ the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic}
gives $b_k\ge e^{L/5}$, which exceeds $L$ once $L\ge13$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow} at the end of this section proves every row.
The consequence
$\Lambda(F)\ge\log L!$ is asymptotically weaker than
Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic}, so the interest is in the individual rows.

\subsection{The rows \texorpdfstring{$k\ge2$}{k at least 2}}

For $u\ge1$ the excluded set $U$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} is arbitrary, so
the distances $W$ on which the certificate must vanish are forced and only
the remaining $L-u-1$ zeros are ours to place.  What Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}
buys is a bound in closed product form that is uniform in $U$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} places the free zeros at the first available
distances; that choice is convenient, not optimal.  Placing them optimally
for each $U$ gives a better bound, the worst case over $U$, with no closed
form in general.  Proposition~\ref{prop:nonsharp} below carries this out
at one root set and improves \eqref{eq:order} there.  First, two root sets
at which the rows are sharp.

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of the row $k=2$ at $(2,3)$]
\label{prop:sharp23}
For $n\ge2$ put
\[
  t_n=\frac{2}{1-2^{1-n}+3^{-n}},
  \qquad a_n=2+t_n\bigl(1-2^{-n}\bigr),
  \qquad
  F_n(x)=x^{n+1}-a_nx^n+t_n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^j .
\]
Then each $F_n$ is a monic multiple of $(x-2)(x-3)$ with $b_2(F_n)=t_n$, and
$t_n$ decreases to $2$.  Hence
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}
  b_2\bigl(Q(x)(x-2)(x-3)\bigr)=2,
\]
the value \eqref{eq:order} asserts for $u=1$.  Every $F_n$ has $b_2>2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Summing the geometric progression, $F_n(2)=0$ and $F_n(3)=0$ read
$a_n=2+t_n(1-2^{-n})$ and $a_n=3+\tfrac12t_n(1-3^{-n})$; the two agree exactly
when $t_n\bigl(\tfrac12-2^{-n}+\tfrac123^{-n}\bigr)=1$, which is the stated
$t_n$.  Writing $d_n=1-2^{1-n}+3^{-n}$, one has $d_n<1$ because
$3^{-n}<2\cdot2^{-n}$, and $d_n\ge\tfrac12$ because $2^{1-n}\le\tfrac12$ for
$n\ge2$, so $2<t_n\le4$; and
$d_{n+1}-d_n=2^{-n}-\tfrac23\cdot3^{-n}>0$, so $d_n$ increases to $1$ and
$t_n$ decreases to $2$.  Finally $a_n-t_n=2-t_n2^{-n}\ge2-4\cdot2^{-n}>0$ for
$n\ge2$, so the nonleading magnitudes are $a_n$ once and $t_n$ exactly $n$
times with $a_n>t_n>0$; thus $b_2(F_n)=t_n$.  Theorem~\ref{thm:order} with
$(r_1,r_2)=(2,3)$ and $u=1$ gives $b_2\ge2$ for every monic multiple, and
$t_n\to2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of the rows $k=2,3$ at $(2,3,5)$]
\label{prop:sharp235}
For $P(x)=(x-2)(x-3)(x-5)$,
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_2(PQ)=8,
  \qquad
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_3(PQ)=4,
\]
the values \eqref{eq:order} gives at $u=1$ and $u=2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The construction of Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp23} adapts.  Build the
multiple from the top down, with a leading $1$, a short free prefix, a long run of one repeated
value, and the exempt coefficients at the bottom:
\[
  F_D(x)=x^D-a_Dx^{D-1}+t_D\sum_{j=1}^{D-2}x^j+c_D,
  \qquad
  G_D(x)=x^D-s_D\sum_{j=2}^{D-1}x^j+q_Dx+q_D',
\]
with the coefficients determined by vanishing at $2,3,5$.  Dividing
$F_D(r)=0$ by $r^{D-1}$ and writing $\gamma_D=c_D2^{1-D}$ gives
\[
  r-a_D+t_D\frac{1-r^{2-D}}{r-1}+\gamma_D\Bigl(\frac2r\Bigr)^{D-1}=0,
  \qquad r\in\{2,3,5\},
\]
a linear system in $(a_D,t_D,\gamma_D)$ whose coefficients converge, as
$D\to\infty$, to those of $2-a+t+\gamma=0$, $3-a+t/2=0$, $5-a+t/4=0$.  That
limit system has determinant $1/4$ and solution $(a,t,\gamma)=(7,8,-3)$, so
$(a_D,t_D,\gamma_D)\to(7,8,-3)$.  Hence $|c_D|\sim3\cdot2^{D-1}$ and
$a_D<t_D$ for large $D$, and the nonleading magnitudes $a_D$, $t_D$ ($D-2$
times) and $|c_D|$ give $b_2(F_D)=t_D\to8$.  Likewise, with
$(\hat q_D,\hat q_D')=3^{-D}(q_D,q_D')$, dividing $G_D(r)=0$ by $r^D$ for $r=3,5$ and by
$3^D$ for $r=2$ gives
\begin{gather*}
  1-s_D\frac{1-r^{2-D}}{r-1}+(r\hat q_D+\hat q_D')\Bigl(\frac3r\Bigr)^D=0
  \qquad(r=3,5),\\
  \Bigl(\frac23\Bigr)^D\bigl(1-s_D(1-2^{2-D})\bigr)+2\hat q_D+\hat q_D'=0,
\end{gather*}
with limit system $1-s/4=0$, $1-s/2+3\hat q+\hat q'=0$, $2\hat q+\hat q'=0$,
of determinant $-1/4$ and solution $(s,\hat q,\hat q')=(4,1,-2)$.  So $s_D\to4$ while $|q_D|\sim3^D$
and $|q_D'|\sim2\cdot3^D$, and $b_3(G_D)=s_D\to4$.  Theorem~\ref{thm:order} gives $b_2\ge8$ and
$b_3\ge4$ for every monic multiple, so both are infima.
\end{proof}

The rows are not infima at every root set.  The next proposition computes
one row exactly and finds it strictly above \eqref{eq:order}; the
certificate machinery supplies both the improved lower bound and the
matching construction.

\begin{proposition}[Non-sharpness of the row $k=2$ at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$]
\label{prop:nonsharp}
Let $P(x)=(x-\tfrac52)(x-3)(x-\tfrac72)$.  Then
\[
  \inf_{\substack{Q\in\R[x]\\ Q\text{ monic}}}b_2(PQ)=\frac{65}{12},
\]
whereas \eqref{eq:order} gives only $b_2\ge(3-1)(\tfrac72-1)=5$.  The
infimum is not attained.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The nodes are $\rho=(\tfrac72,3,\tfrac52)$ and
$y=(\tfrac27,\tfrac13,\tfrac25)$, with $c=3$ and $y_c=\tfrac25\le\tfrac12$.

\emph{Lower bound.}  This is the optimization described at the start of this subsection: the excluded
set $U$ is a single position, and we place the one free zero differently
according to where that position sits, then take the worst case.  Let $F$ be
a monic multiple of $P$ of degree $D$ and let
$j<D$ be a position with $|f_j|=b_1(F)$; put $z=D-j\ge1$.  For any
certificate $w$ on these nodes with $w_0=1$ and $w_z=0$, the identity in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack}, applied with $U=\{j\}$, gives
\[
  1\le\sum_{j'<D,\ j'\ne j}|f_{j'}|\,|w_{D-j'}|\le b_2(F)\Tail(w),
\]
since every position other than $j$ has magnitude at most $b_2(F)$.

If $z\le2$, take $Z=\{1,2\}$.  By Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive},
$\Tail(w)=\prod_iy_i/(1-y_i)=\tfrac25\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac2{15}$,
so $b_2(F)\ge\tfrac{15}2$.

If $z\ge3$, take $Z=\{2,z\}$, so that $Z'=Z\setminus\{z\}=\{2\}$.  The
deletion step in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} gives
$\Tail(w)<\Tail(v)$, where $v$ is the sum on the two nodes
$\tfrac27,\tfrac13$ with $v_0=1$ and $v_2=0$, namely
\[
  v_d=\frac{49}{13}\Bigl(\frac27\Bigr)^{d}-\frac{36}{13}\Bigl(\frac13\Bigr)^{d}.
\]
Being a two-node sum, $v$ has at most one real zero, so $d=2$ is its only
one and $v$ changes sign there: $v_1=\tfrac2{13}>0$ and $v_d<0$ for $d\ge3$.
Since
$\sum_{d\ge1}v_d=\tfrac{49}{13}\cdot\tfrac25-\tfrac{36}{13}\cdot\tfrac12
=\tfrac8{65}$, and $v_2=0$,
\[
  \Tail(v)=2v_1-\sum_{d\ge1}v_d=\frac4{13}-\frac8{65}=\frac{12}{65},
\]
whence $b_2(F)>\tfrac{65}{12}$.  As $\tfrac{65}{12}<\tfrac{15}2$, every monic
multiple has $b_2(F)>\tfrac{65}{12}$, and the infimum is not attained.

\emph{Matching construction.}  For $D\ge6$ let
\[
  F_D(x)=x^D-h_Dx^{D-1}+a_Dx^{D-2}+h_D\sum_{j=1}^{D-3}x^j+c_D,
\]
with $(h_D,a_D,c_D)$ determined by $F_D(\tfrac52)=F_D(3)=F_D(\tfrac72)=0$.
Dividing each equation by $r^D$ and writing $\gamma_D=c_D(\tfrac25)^D$, the
coefficient of $h_D$ contributes
$-r^{-1}+\bigl(r^{-2}-r^{1-D}\bigr)/(r-1)$ and $\gamma_D$ enters only through
$\bigl(5/(2r)\bigr)^D$, which tends to $1$ at $r=\tfrac52$ and to $0$ at
$r=3,\tfrac72$.  The limiting system is therefore
\[
  1-\frac{2h}5+\frac{4a}{25}+\frac{8h}{75}+\gamma=0,\qquad
  1-\frac h3+\frac a9+\frac h{18}=0,\qquad
  1-\frac{2h}7+\frac{4a}{49}+\frac{8h}{245}=0 .
\]
The last two give $2a=5h-18$ and $245-62h+20a=0$, hence $65-12h=0$; solving,
\[
  (h,a,\gamma)=\Bigl(\frac{65}{12},\ \frac{109}{24},\ -\frac{31}{225}\Bigr),
\]
and the system is nonsingular, so $(h_D,a_D,\gamma_D)$ converges to it.  Since
$\gamma\ne0$ we have $|c_D|\sim\tfrac{31}{225}(\tfrac52)^D\to\infty$, and
$a\to\tfrac{109}{24}<\tfrac{65}{12}$.  So for all large $D$ the nonleading
magnitudes are $|c_D|$ once, $h_D$ exactly $D-2$ times and $a_D<h_D$ once,
with $|c_D|>h_D$; hence $b_2(F_D)=h_D\to\tfrac{65}{12}$.
\end{proof}

So sharpness of the rows $k\ge2$ depends on the root set: it holds at
$(2,3)$ and $(2,3,5)$ and fails at $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$.  The companion
paper~\cite{coefficient-mass-attainment} characterizes the root sets at
which \eqref{eq:order} is an infimum.  For $2\le r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$,
repeated roots included, the row $k=u+1$ is the infimum of $b_{u+1}(PQ)$
over monic $Q$ exactly when no partial sum of the coefficients of
$P_u=\prod_{i>u}(x-r_i)$, read down from the leading one, exceeds their
total $P_u(1)$ in absolute value.  At $(\tfrac52,3,\tfrac72)$ with $u=1$ the
first partial sum of $(x-3)(x-\tfrac72)$ is $-\tfrac{11}2$, against
$P_1(1)=5$.  When the criterion fails and the roots are distinct, that paper
also evaluates the infimum, which then depends on $r_{u+1},\ldots,r_L$
alone.

\subsection{A \texorpdfstring{$D$}{D}-free form of the rows}

We return to the rows $b_k\ge L-k+1$ for monic multiples of
$(x-2)^L$ from the third remark after Corollary~\ref{cor:allroots}.  At a
single root of multiplicity $L$ the certificates of Section~3 are replaced
by their confluent limits, polynomials in $s$ times $2^{-s}$, and the
degree $D$ drops out of the problem.  For a finite set $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ put
\[
  r_Z(s)=\prod_{z\in Z}\Bigl(1-\frac sz\Bigr),\qquad
  \Phi(Z)=\sum_{s\ge1}|r_Z(s)|\,2^{-s},
\]
so $r_Z(0)=1$, $\deg r_Z=|Z|$, and $\Phi(\varnothing)=1$.  The rows reduce to
the following statement about $\Phi$.

\begin{lemma}[Certificate for a row]\label{lem:rowcert}
Let $F$ be a monic multiple of $(x-2)^L$ of degree $D$, let $1\le k\le L$,
let $S$ be the set of backward distances $D-j$ of $k-1$ positions $j<D$
carrying the $k-1$ largest nonleading magnitudes, and put $n=L-k+1$.  If
some finite $Z\supseteq S$ with $|Z|\le L-1$ has $\Phi(Z)\le1/n$, then
$b_k(F)\ge n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $\theta=x\,d/dx$ and $q=r_Z$, of degree at most $L-1$.  Since
$q(D-\theta)x^j=q(D-j)x^j$ and each application of $\theta$ lowers the order
of the zero of $F$ at $2$ by at most one, $(q(D-\theta)F)(2)=0$, that is
$\sum_{j\le D}f_jq(D-j)2^{j}=0$.  Dividing by $2^D$ and using $f_D=q(0)=1$
and $q=0$ on $S$,
\[
  1=\Bigl|\sum_{1\le s\le D,\ s\notin S}f_{D-s}\,q(s)2^{-s}\Bigr|
   \le b_k(F)\sum_{s\ge1}|q(s)|2^{-s}=b_k(F)\,\Phi(Z)\le\frac{b_k(F)}n,
\]
because every position outside the $k-1$ largest has magnitude at most
$b_k(F)$.
\end{proof}

So row $k$ at $L$ follows, for every $D$, from the statement $T(n)$ with
$n=L-k+1$: \emph{for every finite $A\subset\N_{>0}$ there is a finite
$Z\supseteq A$ with $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$ and $\Phi(Z)\le1/n$.}  Two tools
evaluate $\Phi$.

\begin{lemma}[Deleting the largest zero]\label{lem:confdel}
If $Z\ne\varnothing$ and $Z\ne\{1,\ldots,|Z|\}$, then
$\Phi(Z)\le\Phi(Z\setminus\{\max Z\})$.  Moreover
$\Phi(\{1,\ldots,\ell\})=1$ for every $\ell\ge0$, so $\Phi(Z)\le1$ for every
$Z$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $c=|Z|+1$ and $Z'=Z\setminus\{\max Z\}$.  For small $\varepsilon>0$ take
the nodes $y_i=\tfrac12-(c-i)\varepsilon$, so $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c=\tfrac12$,
let $w^\varepsilon$ be the sum on $y_1,\ldots,y_c$ with $w_0=1$ and
$w|_Z=0$, and $F^\varepsilon$ the sum on $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with $F_0=1$
and $F|_{Z'}=0$.  As $Z$ is not $\{1,\ldots,c-1\}$, the deletion step in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} gives $\Tail(w^\varepsilon)<\Tail(F^\varepsilon)$.

Now let $\varepsilon\to0$.  Write $w^\varepsilon_d=\sum_{i=1}^c\alpha_i
[y_1,\ldots,y_i](y^d)$ in Newton form, the bracket being the divided
difference of $y\mapsto y^d$, equal to $h_{d-i+1}(y_1,\ldots,y_i)$.  The
$c$ interpolation conditions at $d\in\{0\}\cup Z$ form a linear system for
$\alpha$ whose matrix tends to the matrix with entries
$\binom d{i-1}2^{-(d-i+1)}$; that limit is invertible, because
$\binom d{i-1}$, $i\le c$, is a basis of the polynomials of degree below
$c$ and such a polynomial vanishing at the $c$ points $\{0\}\cup Z$ is zero.
Hence $\alpha$ stays bounded and converges, and since
$0\le h_{d-i+1}(y_1,\ldots,y_i)\le\binom d{i-1}2^{-(d-i+1)}$, the terms
$|w^\varepsilon_d|$ are dominated by $Cd^{\,c}2^{-d}$.  The limit
$w^0_d=p(d)2^{-d}$ has $\deg p<c$, $p(0)=1$ and $p|_Z=0$, so $p=r_Z$, and by
dominated convergence $\Tail(w^\varepsilon)\to\Phi(Z)$.  Likewise
$\Tail(F^\varepsilon)\to\Phi(Z')$, which proves the inequality.

Finally $|r_{\{1,\ldots,\ell\}}(s)|=\binom{s-1}\ell$ for $s>\ell$ and $0$ for
$1\le s\le\ell$, and $\sum_{s>\ell}\binom{s-1}\ell2^{-s}=1$.  Deleting the
largest element repeatedly from any $Z$ stops at its leading run
$\{1,\ldots,\ell\}$, never passing through an initial segment before, so
$\Phi(Z)\le1$.
\end{proof}

The last clause is the row $k=L$ ($n=1$) in $D$-free form.  For the other
rows we compute $\Phi$ exactly on sets that are full up to a few holes.

\begin{lemma}[Hole integral]\label{lem:holeint}
Let $C\subset\N_{>0}$ be finite and nonempty, $m=\max C$, and for
$c\in C$ put $w_c=\prod_{c'\in C}c'\big/\prod_{c'\in C\setminus\{c\}}(c-c')$.
For every $x\ge m$,
\[
  \Phi\bigl(\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus C\bigr)=\int_0^1v^{x-m}M_C(v)\,dv,
  \qquad
  M_C(v)=\sum_{c\in C}2^{-c}v^{m-c}\bigl(w_c(1+v)^{c-1}+|w_c|(1-v)^{c-1}\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $P=\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus C$ and $r=r_P$.  For $s>x$ every factor of
$r(s)$ is negative and
$|r(s)|=\binom{s-1}x\prod_{c\in C}\frac c{s-c}=\binom{s-1}x\sum_{c\in C}\frac{w_c}{s-c}$,
the last step by partial fractions.  Using $\frac1{s-c}=\int_0^1u^{s-c-1}du$,
$\sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}x y^s=y^{x+1}(1-y)^{-x-1}$ and then $v=u/(2-u)$,
\[
  \sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}x\frac{2^{-s}}{s-c}
  =\int_0^1\frac{u^{x-c}\,du}{(2-u)^{x+1}}
  =2^{-c}\int_0^1v^{x-c}(1+v)^{c-1}\,dv .
\]
For $s\le x$, $r(s)=0$ unless $s\in C$, and for $c\in C$,
\[
  |r(c)|=\frac{\prod_{p\le x,\,p\ne c}|p-c|/p}{\prod_{c'\in C\setminus\{c\}}|c'-c|/c'}
  =\frac1{\binom xc}\cdot\frac{|w_c|}c
  =|w_c|\int_0^1v^{x-c}(1-v)^{c-1}\,dv .
\]
Summing the finitely many series gives the formula.
\end{proof}

For $C=\{1\}$ and $C=\{2\}$ one finds $M_C\equiv1$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:onehole}
  \Phi(\{2,\ldots,x\})=\frac1x,\qquad
  \Phi(\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus\{2\})=\frac1{x-1};
\end{equation}
the first is the identity
$\sum_{s\ge1}|\prod_{z=2}^n(1-s/z)|2^{-s}=1/n$ behind
Proposition~\ref{prop:unbounded}.  For $C=\{3,b\}$ with $b\ge4$,
$w_b=-w_3=3b/(b-3)$, and with $(1+v)^2-(1-v)^2=4v$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twohole}
  M_{\{3,b\}}(v)=\frac{3b}{b-3}\Bigl(2^{-b}\bigl((1+v)^{b-1}+(1-v)^{b-1}\bigr)
                   -\tfrac12v^{b-2}\Bigr).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Two holes]\label{lem:twohole}
For $b\ge4$ and $k\ge0$,
$\Phi(\{1,\ldots,b+k\}\setminus\{3,b\})\le1/(k+1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $b=4$, \eqref{eq:twohole} is $\tfrac32(1-v^2)$, and
$\int_0^1v^k\tfrac32(1-v^2)\,dv=\frac3{(k+1)(k+3)}\le\frac1{k+1}$.  Let
$b\ge5$ and expand $M=M_{\{3,b\}}$ in the Bernstein basis
$B_i=\binom{b-1}iv^i(1-v)^{b-1-i}$, $0\le i\le b-1$.  From
$1+v=(1-v)+2v$ we get
$2^{-b}(1+v)^{b-1}=\sum_i2^{-(b-i)}B_i$; also $(1-v)^{b-1}=B_0$ and
$v^{b-2}=B_{b-1}+B_{b-2}/(b-1)$.  So $M=\frac{3b}{b-3}\sum_i\gamma_iB_i$ with
\[
  \gamma_0=2^{1-b},\quad
  \gamma_i=2^{-(b-i)}\ (1\le i\le b-3),\quad
  \gamma_{b-2}=\frac{b-3}{4(b-1)},\quad
  \gamma_{b-1}=0 .
\]
Every coefficient $\frac{3b}{b-3}\gamma_i$ is at most $1$: the largest
candidates are $\frac{3b}{8(b-3)}\le1$ (as $b\ge5$) and $\frac{3b}{4(b-1)}\le1$.
Since the $B_i$ are nonnegative and sum to $1$, $0\le M\le1$ on $[0,1]$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint} gives
$\Phi\le\int_0^1v^k\,dv=1/(k+1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rows away from the top three positions]\label{prop:rowsfree}
Let $n\ge1$ and let $A\subset\N_{>0}$ be finite with
$\{1,2,3\}\not\subseteq A$.  Then some finite $Z\supseteq A$ has
$|Z|\le|A|+n-1$ and $\Phi(Z)\le1/n$.  Consequently, if $F$ is a monic
multiple of $(x-2)^L$ of degree $D$ and $1\le k\le L$, and the $k-1$ largest
nonleading magnitudes can be chosen at positions not including all of
$D-1,D-2,D-3$, then $b_k(F)\ge L-k+1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n=1$ take $Z=A$ and use Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel}.  Let $n\ge2$, let $j$
be the leading run of $A$ (so $j\le2$), and let $c_1=j+1<c_2<\cdots$ be the
positive integers outside $A$.  Keep the holes
$K=\{1\}$, $\{2\}$, $\{3,c_2\}$ for $j=0,1,2$, fill the next $n-1$ of them,
and put
\[
  x=c_{|K|+n-1},\qquad P=\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus K,\qquad Z=P\cup A .
\]
Then $P\supseteq A\cap[1,x]$, the elements of $Z\setminus A$ are the $n-1$
filled holes, and $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$.

\emph{From $Z$ to $P$.}  List the elements of $Z$ above $x$ increasingly and
add them to $P$ one at a time.  Every set so obtained has the hole
$\min K\le3$ below an element larger than it, so it is not of the form
$\{1,\ldots,t\}$; reading the additions backwards, Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel}
gives $\Phi(Z)\le\Phi(P)$.

\emph{The base.}  For $j=0$, $P=\{2,\ldots,x\}$ with $x=c_n\ge n$, and
\eqref{eq:onehole} gives $\Phi(P)=1/x\le1/n$.  For $j=1$,
$P=\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus\{2\}$ with $x=c_n\ge n+1$, and
$\Phi(P)=1/(x-1)\le1/n$.  For $j=2$ put $b=c_2\ge4$; the $n-1$ filled holes
exceed $b$, so $x\ge b+n-1$.  The sets
$\{1,\ldots,t\}\setminus\{3,b\}$, $b+n-1\le t\le x$, contain $b+1$ and miss
$3$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel} and Lemma~\ref{lem:twohole} with $k=n-1$ give
$\Phi(P)\le\Phi(\{1,\ldots,b+n-1\}\setminus\{3,b\})\le1/n$.

The consequence is Lemma~\ref{lem:rowcert} with $A=S$: here $|S|=k-1$, so
$|Z|\le L-1$, and $\{1,2,3\}\not\subseteq S$ is the hypothesis on the
positions.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The top three positions.}
There remains $T(n)$ for $A\supseteq\{1,2,3\}$, that is, a leading run
$j\ge3$.  The construction is the one above with the first $j+1$ holes kept.
The two parts of $\Phi$ that appear in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint}
are bounded separately: the values at the kept holes by comparison with the
consecutive block $\{j+1,\ldots,2j+1\}$, and the tail beyond $x$ by a Beta
integral.  Throughout, $j\ge0$, $C=\{c_1<\cdots<c_{j+1}\}\subset\N_{>0}$ with
$c_1=j+1$, $m=c_{j+1}$, $k\ge0$, $x=m+k$, $P=\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus C$ and
$r=r_P$.  Then $m\ge2j+1$, and by the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint}
$\Phi(P)=H(C,k)+T(C,k)$, where
\[
  H(C,k)=\sum_{c\in C}|r(c)|\,2^{-c},\qquad
  T(C,k)=\sum_{s>x}|r(s)|\,2^{-s}
        =\sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}x2^{-s}\prod_{c\in C}\frac c{s-c}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Values at the kept holes]\label{lem:holevals}
$H(C,k)\le\dfrac1{2(k+1)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $1\le i\le j+1$, put $c=c_i$ and $i'=j+1-i$, the number of kept holes
above $c$, and write $|r(c)|=\prod_{p\in P}|p-c|/p$.

\emph{Factors below $c$.}  $P\cap[1,c)$ consists of $\{1,\ldots,j\}$ and a
set $\Lambda\subseteq\{j+2,\ldots,c-1\}$ that avoids the $i-2$ holes
$c_2,\ldots,c_{i-1}$, so $|\Lambda|=a:=c-j-i$.  The factor $(c-p)/p$ is
positive and decreasing in $p$ on $p<c$, so the product over $\Lambda$ is at
most the product over the $a$ smallest candidates $j+2,\ldots,j+1+a$.
Together with $p\le j$ this is $\prod_{p=1}^{q}(c-p)/p=\binom{c-1}q$ at
$q=j+1+a=c-i+1$, divided by the factor $(c-j-1)/(j+1)$ of the missing
$p=j+1$; as $\binom{c-1}{c-i+1}=\binom{c-1}{i-2}$, this gives
\[
  \prod_{p\in P,\,p<c}\frac{c-p}p\le\lambda_i(c):=
  \frac{j+1}{c-j-1}\binom{c-1}{i-2}\quad(i\ge2),\qquad\lambda_1(j+1)=1,
\]
the second because for $i=1$ the product is
$\prod_{p=1}^{j}(j+1-p)/p=1$.

\emph{Factors above $c$.}  $P\cap(c,x]$ is $(c,x]$ with the $i'$ holes
$c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{j+1}$ removed.  The factor $(p-c)/p$ lies in $(0,1)$ and
increases with $p$, so the product is at most the product over the
$x-c-i'$ largest integers of $(c,x]$, which is
$\prod_{p=c+i'+1}^{x}(p-c)/p=\binom{c+i'}c\big/\binom xc$.  Since
$m\ge c+i'$, we have $x\ge c+i'+k$, and hence
\[
  2^{-c}|r(c)|\le U_i(c):=2^{-c}\lambda_i(c)
        \binom{c+i'}c\Big/\binom{c+i'+k}c .
\]

\emph{Moving $c$ down.}  For $i\ge2$ and $c\ge j+i$,
\[
  \frac{U_i(c+1)}{U_i(c)}
  =\frac{c-j-1}{2(c-j)}\cdot\frac c{c-i+2}\cdot\frac{c+i'+1}{c+i'+k+1}
  \le\frac c{2(c-i+2)}\le1,
\]
the last step because $c\ge j+i\ge2i-1$ (as $i\le j+1$).  Since
$c_i\ge j+i$, we get $2^{-c_i}|r(c_i)|\le U_i(j+i)$ for every $i$; for $i=1$
this is the bound itself.

\emph{The block.}  For $C^\circ=\{j+1,\ldots,2j+1\}$ with the same $k$, all
three bounds are equalities: $\Lambda=\varnothing$, the holes above
$c_i^\circ=j+i$ are $j+i+1,\ldots,2j+1$, and $x^\circ=2j+1+k=c_i^\circ+i'+k$.
So $2^{-c_i}|r_P(c_i)|\le2^{-c_i^\circ}|r_{P^\circ}(c_i^\circ)|$ term by term,
and $H(C,k)\le H(C^\circ,k)$.  By the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint},
$H(C^\circ,k)=\int_0^1v^k\sum_{c\in C^\circ}|w_c|2^{-c}v^{2j+1-c}(1-v)^{c-1}\,dv$
with $|w_{j+i}|=(2j+1)!/\bigl(j!\,(i-1)!\,(j+1-i)!\bigr)$, and the binomial
theorem gives
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^{j+1}|w_{j+i}|2^{-j-i}v^{j+1-i}(1-v)^{j+i-1}
  =\frac{(2j+1)!}{j!^2\,2^{j+1}}(1-v)^j\Bigl(v+\frac{1-v}2\Bigr)^j
  =\frac{\gamma_j}2(1-v^2)^j,
\]
where $\gamma_j=(2j+1)!/(j!^24^j)$ satisfies
$\int_0^1\gamma_j(1-v^2)^j\,dv=1$.  The function $(1-v^2)^j$ is
nonincreasing and $v^k$ is nondecreasing, so Chebyshev's integral inequality
gives $H(C^\circ,k)\le\frac12\int_0^1v^k\,dv=\frac1{2(k+1)}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The tail beyond the kept holes]\label{lem:holetail}
If $j\ge1$ and $k\ge1$, then $T(C,k)\le\dfrac{j+1}{e\,j\,(k+1)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $s>x$.  Every $c\in C$ is below $s$, and $c/(s-c)$ increases with $c$.
Since $c_1=j+1$ and $c_i\le m-(j+1-i)$ for $i\ge2$,
\[
  \prod_{c\in C}\frac c{s-c}
  \le\frac{j+1}{s-j-1}\prod_{l=1}^{j}\frac{m-j+l}{s-m+j-l}
  =\frac{j+1}{s-j-1}\binom mj\Big/\binom{s-m-1+j}j .
\]
Apply $1/\binom Nj=(N+1)\int_0^1t^j(1-t)^{N-j}\,dt$ with $N=s-m-1+j$, and
note that $N+1=s-m+j\le s-j-1$ because $m\ge2j+1$:
\[
  \prod_{c\in C}\frac c{s-c}\le(j+1)\binom mj\int_0^1t^j(1-t)^{s-m-1}\,dt .
\]
Multiply by $\binom{s-1}x2^{-s}$ and sum over $s>x$.  With
$\sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}xy^s=\bigl(y/(1-y)\bigr)^{x+1}$ at $y=(1-t)/2$, the
sum of $\binom{s-1}x2^{-s}(1-t)^{s-m-1}$ is
$(1-t)^{-m-1}\bigl((1-t)/(1+t)\bigr)^{x+1}$, so
\[
  T(C,k)\le(j+1)\binom mj\int_0^1t^j
     \Bigl(\frac{1-t}{1+t}\Bigr)^k(1+t)^{-m-1}\,dt .
\]
Now $(1-t)/(1+t)\le e^{-2t}$, since $\log\frac{1+t}{1-t}\ge2t$, and for
$k\ge1$, $(k+1)e^{-2kt}\le2k\,e^{-2kt}\le1/(et)$, since $ye^{-y}\le1/e$.
Extending the integral to $(0,\infty)$,
\[
  (k+1)\,T(C,k)\le\frac{j+1}e\binom mj\int_0^\infty t^{j-1}(1+t)^{-m-1}\,dt
  =\frac{j+1}e\binom mj\,\mathrm B(j,m-j+1)=\frac{j+1}{e\,j}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rows at the top three positions]\label{prop:rowstop}
Let $n\ge1$ and let $A\subset\N_{>0}$ be finite with $\{1,2,3\}\subseteq A$.
Then some finite $Z\supseteq A$ has $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$ and $\Phi(Z)\le1/n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n=1$ take $Z=A$ and use Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel}.  Let $n\ge2$, let
$j\ge3$ be the leading run of $A$, and let $c_1=j+1<c_2<\cdots$ be the
positive integers outside $A$.  Keep $C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_{j+1}\}$, fill the
next $n-1$ holes, and put
\[
  x=c_{j+n},\qquad P=\{1,\ldots,x\}\setminus C,\qquad Z=P\cup A .
\]
Then $P\supseteq A\cap[1,x]$, the elements of $Z\setminus A$ are the $n-1$
filled holes $c_{j+2},\ldots,c_{j+n}$, and $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$.  As in the proof
of Proposition~\ref{prop:rowsfree}, adding the elements of $Z$ above $x$ to
$P$ one at a time never produces a set $\{1,\ldots,t\}$, because the hole
$j+1$ lies below them, so Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel} gives
$\Phi(Z)\le\Phi(P)$.  Put $m=c_{j+1}$ and $k=x-m\ge n-1\ge1$.  By
Lemmas~\ref{lem:holevals} and~\ref{lem:holetail},
\[
  \Phi(P)=H(C,k)+T(C,k)\le\frac1{k+1}\Bigl(\frac12+\frac{j+1}{e\,j}\Bigr)
  \le\frac1{k+1}\Bigl(\frac12+\frac4{3e}\Bigr)<\frac1{k+1}\le\frac1n,
\]
because $(j+1)/j\le4/3$ for $j\ge3$ and $8<3e$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Every row at the root $2$]\label{thm:everyrow}
Let $L\ge1$ and let $F$ be a monic multiple of $(x-2)^L$.  Then
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ L-k+1\qquad(1\le k\le L).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $\deg F\ge L\ge k$, there are at least $k$ nonleading positions.  Let
$S$ be the set of backward distances of $k-1$ positions carrying the $k-1$
largest nonleading magnitudes, and $n=L-k+1$.  Proposition~\ref{prop:rowsfree}
if $\{1,2,3\}\not\subseteq S$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:rowstop} otherwise,
give $Z\supseteq S$ with $|Z|\le|S|+n-1=L-1$ and $\Phi(Z)\le1/n$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:rowcert} gives $b_k(F)\ge n$.
\end{proof}

The row $k=L$ is the trivial $b_L\ge1$, and summing the rows gives only
$\Lambda(F)\ge\log L!$, which Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic} already
exceeds for large $L$; the content of Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow} is the
individual rows.  The bound $\frac12+\frac{j+1}{ej}$ in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rowstop} is far from tight: in exact arithmetic the
largest value of $(k+1)\Phi(P)$ with $j\ge3$ and $k\ge1$ that we found is
$\frac{35}{64}=0.546\ldots$, at the block $C=\{4,5,6,7\}$ with $k=1$.  For
$j\le2$ the
tail bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:holetail} exceeds $\frac12$ and the argument
does not close; that is why Proposition~\ref{prop:rowsfree} keeps different
holes there.

\subsection{Rows at other roots}

We now replace the root $2$ by an arbitrary real $r>1$.  Throughout this
subsection
\[
  a=\frac1{r-1},\qquad
  \Phi_r(q)=\sum_{s\ge1}|q(s)|\,r^{-s},\qquad
  \Phi_r(Z)=\Phi_r(r_Z),\qquad
  \psi_r(y)=\frac{1+a^y}{ry}\quad(y\ge1),
\]
for real polynomials $q$ and finite $Z\subset\N_{>0}$, so $\Phi_2=\Phi$ and
$\Phi_r(\varnothing)=a$.  Write $[u,v]$ for the integers in that range.  For
$1\le k\le L$ put $n=L-k+1$ and
\[
  V_r(L,k)=\inf\{b_k(F): F\text{ a monic real multiple of }(x-r)^L\},
\]
the exact value of the row.  For finite $S\subset\N_{>0}$ with $|S|<L$ let
$\mu_r(S,L)$ be the infimum of $\Phi_r(q)$ over real $q$ with $\deg q\le L-1$,
$q(0)=1$ and $q|_S=0$, and put $\beta_r(n)=1/\mu_r(\varnothing,n)$.  At
$r=2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow} says $V_2(L,k)\ge n$.

\begin{proposition}[The row values]\label{prop:rowvalue}
Let $r>1$ and $1\le k\le L$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $F$ is a monic multiple of $(x-r)^L$, $S$ is the set of
backward distances of $k-1$ positions carrying the $k-1$ largest nonleading
magnitudes, and $q$ is real with $\deg q\le L-1$, $q(0)=1$ and $q|_S=0$, then
$b_k(F)\,\Phi_r(q)\ge1$.  In particular $b_k(F)\ge1/\Phi_r(Z)$ for every
finite $Z\supseteq S$ with $|Z|\le L-1$.
\item[(b)] $V_r(L,k)=\inf_{|S|=k-1}1/\mu_r(S,L)$, the infimum over
$S\subset\N_{>0}$.
\item[(c)] $V_r(L,k)\le\beta_r(n)$, and $V_r(n,1)=\beta_r(n)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) This is the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:rowcert} with $2$ replaced by $r$:
$(q(D-\theta)F)(r)=0$ with $\theta=x\,d/dx$ gives
$\sum_{j\le D}f_jq(D-j)r^{j-D}=0$, so
$1\le\sum_{s\ge1,\,s\notin S}|f_{D-s}||q(s)|r^{-s}\le b_k(F)\Phi_r(q)$.

(b) By (a), $b_k(F)\ge1/\mu_r(S_F,L)$ for the set $S_F$ of (a), which
gives ``$\ge$''.  For ``$\le$'' fix $S$ with $|S|=k-1$ and $D\ge\max(L,\max S)$.
Writing $F=\sum_{s=0}^Dg_sx^{D-s}$ with $g_0=1$, divisibility by
$(x-r)^L$ is the set of $L$ linear conditions
$\sum_{s=0}^Dg_sq(s)r^{-s}=0$ for $\deg q\le L-1$ (as in (a), these are the
conditions $(\theta^iF)(r)=0$, $i<L$).  Minimizing
$\max_{s\in[1,D]\setminus S}|g_s|$ over this affine set is a finite linear
program, feasible because $D\ge L$, and by linear-programming duality its value is
$1/\mu_{r,D}(S,L)$, where $\mu_{r,D}$ is defined like $\mu_r$ with the sum
$\Phi_r(q)$ truncated to $s\le D$ (the dual variables are the values
$q(s)r^{-s}$ of a polynomial $q$ of degree at most $L-1$ vanishing on $S$;
for weak duality, $|q(0)|=|\sum_{s\ge1,\,s\notin S}g_sq(s)r^{-s}|$).  Since
the $k-1$ positions at distances $S$ can carry at most the $k-1$ largest
magnitudes, $b_k(F)\le\max_{s\notin S}|g_s|$, so
$V_r(L,k)\le1/\mu_{r,D}(S,L)$ for every $D$.  Finally
$\mu_{r,D}(S,L)\to\mu_r(S,L)$: the truncated sums increase with $D$ and
are at most $\Phi_r$; on the affine space $\{\deg q\le L-1,\ q(0)=1\}$ the
values $q(1),\ldots,q(L-1)$ are coordinates, so minimizers $q_D$ of the
truncated problems ($D\ge L$) have bounded coefficients, and a limit point
$q_*$ satisfies $q_*(0)=1$, $q_*|_S=0$ and, for each fixed $M$,
$\sum_{s\le M}|q_*(s)|r^{-s}\le\lim_D\mu_{r,D}(S,L)$; letting $M\to\infty$
gives $\mu_r(S,L)\le\Phi_r(q_*)\le\lim_D\mu_{r,D}(S,L)$.

(c) Let $S_N=[N+1,N+k-1]$.  A polynomial $q$ of degree at most $L-1$ with
$q(0)=1$ and $q|_{S_N}=0$ factors as $q=q_0\pi_N$ with
$\pi_N(s)=\prod_{\sigma\in S_N}(1-s/\sigma)$, $\deg q_0\le n-1$ and
$q_0(0)=1$.  For $1\le s\le M<N$ we have $\pi_N(s)\ge(1-M/N)^{k-1}$, so
$\Phi_r(q)\ge(1-M/N)^{k-1}\mu_{r,M}(\varnothing,n)$.  Hence
$\liminf_N\mu_r(S_N,L)\ge\mu_{r,M}(\varnothing,n)$ for every $M$, and by
the convergence in (b) it is at least $\mu_r(\varnothing,n)$.  By (b),
$V_r(L,k)\le\inf_N1/\mu_r(S_N,L)\le\beta_r(n)$.  For $k=1$, (b) with
$S=\varnothing$ is $V_r(n,1)=\beta_r(n)$.
\end{proof}

So every row is at most the top row of a multiple of $(x-r)^n$, the value
approached when the $k-1$ exempted positions lie far below the leading one.
The lower bounds come from certificates, and the first tool is the
displaced-zero tail bound without the cutoff $\rho_i\ge2$.

\begin{theorem}[Tail bound at arbitrary nodes]\label{thm:tailgen}
Let $0<y_1<\cdots<y_c<1$, let $Z\subset\N_{>0}$ with $|Z|=c-1$ have leading
run $\ell$, let $w$ be the exponential sum on these nodes with $w_0=1$ and
$w|_Z=0$, and put $\varphi_i=y_i/(1-y_i)$.  Then
\[
  \Tail(w)\le\prod_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\varphi_i\prod_{i=\ell+2}^c\max(1,\varphi_i).
\]
Moreover, if $Z\ne[1,c-1]$ and $F$ is the sum on $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ with
$F_0=1$ and $F|_{Z\setminus\{\max Z\}}=0$, then
$\Tail(w)\le\max(1,\varphi_c)\Tail(F)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sum $w$ exists and is unique for any distinct positive nodes, by the
Vandermonde argument at the start of Section~2.  Induct on $c-1-\ell$ as in
the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}.  The base case $Z=[1,c-1]$ is
Lemma~\ref{lem:consecutive}, which gives $\prod_{i=1}^c\varphi_i$ and needs
no cutoff.  In the displaced step, the sign comparison, the identity
\eqref{eq:split} and the first inequality of \eqref{eq:gamma},
$\Gamma_G(z)\le\Gamma_F(z)/(1-y_c)$, use only the zero bound,
Lemma~\ref{lem:interlace} (which needs no upper bound on the nodes) and
$y_c<1$.  If $y_c\le\frac12$, the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tail} gives
$\Tail(w)<\Tail(F)$.  If $y_c>\frac12$, then $\Sigma_G\ge0$ and
\eqref{eq:split} give
\[
  \Tail(w)\le\Tail(F)+\mu\Gamma_F(z)\Bigl(\frac1{1-y_c}-2\Bigr),
\]
where the bracket is positive and
$\mu\Gamma_F(z)=\sum_{d\ge z}|F_d|\le\Tail(F)$ (if $F_z=0$ then $w=F$), so
$\Tail(w)\le(\frac1{1-y_c}-1)\Tail(F)=\varphi_c\Tail(F)$.  In both cases
$\Tail(w)\le\max(1,\varphi_c)\Tail(F)$.  The set $Z\setminus\{\max Z\}$ has
the same leading run $\ell$ and $\ell+1\le c-1$, so the induction hypothesis
for $F$ on $y_1,\ldots,y_{c-1}$ gives the bound.
\end{proof}

For $y_c\le\frac12$ every $\max(1,\varphi_i)$ is $1$ and this is
Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}.  The order statistics extend to all roots above $1$.

\begin{corollary}[Order statistics for roots above one]\label{cor:ordergen}
Let $1<r_1\le\cdots\le r_L$ and let $F$ be a nonzero real multiple of
$\prod_{i=1}^L(x-r_i)$.  Then for every $0\le u\le L-1$,
\[
  b_{u+1}(F)\ge|f_D|\prod_{i=u+1}^L(r_i-1)\prod_{i=1}^u\min(1,r_i-1).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Repeat the proofs of Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} and Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  In
Lemma~\ref{lem:slack} the reciprocal nodes $y_1<\cdots<y_L$ are the
$1/r_i$ in decreasing order of $r_i$ and the leading run of $Z$ is
$\ell\ge L-u-1$; Theorem~\ref{thm:tailgen}, together with
$\varphi_i\le\max(1,\varphi_i)$ for $L-u<i\le\ell+1$, bounds the tail by
$\prod_{i\le L-u}\varphi_i\prod_{i>L-u}\max(1,\varphi_i)$, which is the
reciprocal of the right side (with $|f_D|=1$) because $\varphi_i=1/(\rho_i-1)$.
The perturbation of Theorem~\ref{thm:order} applies unchanged, the bound
being continuous in the roots.
\end{proof}

For $r_1\ge2$ this is Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  At a single root
$r\in(1,2]$ it gives $b_k\ge(r-1)^L$ in every row, which is sharp in the
last row (Corollary~\ref{cor:lastrowr}).

\begin{lemma}[Deleting the largest zero at the root $r$]\label{lem:confdelr}
Let $r>1$.  Then $\Phi_r([1,\ell])=a^{\ell+1}$ for $\ell\ge0$, and for every
nonempty finite $Z$,
\[
  \Phi_r(Z)\le\max(1,a)\,\Phi_r\bigl(Z\setminus\{\max Z\}\bigr).
\]
Consequently $\Phi_r(Z)\le\Phi_r(Z\cap[1,t])$ for all $t$ when $r\ge2$, and
$\Phi_r(Z)\le a^{|Z|+1}$ when $1<r\le2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$|r_{[1,\ell]}(s)|=\binom{s-1}\ell$ for $s>\ell$ and
$\sum_{s>\ell}\binom{s-1}\ell r^{-s}=(r^{-1}/(1-r^{-1}))^{\ell+1}=a^{\ell+1}$;
this also settles $Z=[1,\ell]$, the ratio being $a$.  For
$Z\ne[1,|Z|]$ run the limit in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:confdel} with
the nodes $y_i=\frac1r-(c-i)\varepsilon$ and the comparison
$\Tail(w^\varepsilon)\le\max(1,\varphi_c)\Tail(F^\varepsilon)$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:tailgen}; the domination
$0\le h_{d-i+1}(y_1,\ldots,y_i)\le\binom d{i-1}r^{-(d-i+1)}$ and the
invertibility of the limit matrix are as there, and $\varphi_c\to a$.  The
consequences follow by deleting the largest element repeatedly: for $r\ge2$
each step does not increase $\Phi_r$, and for $r\le2$ each of the $|Z|$
steps from $Z$ to $\varnothing$ costs at most the factor $a$, with
$\Phi_r(\varnothing)=a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The last row]\label{cor:lastrowr}
$V_r(L,L)=r-1$ for $r\ge2$, and $V_r(L,L)=(r-1)^L$ for $1<r\le2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Lower bounds: Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(a) with $Z=S$, $|S|=L-1$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr}, which gives $\Phi_r(S)\le a$ for $r\ge2$ and
$\Phi_r(S)\le a^L$ for $r\le2$.  Upper bounds: for $r\ge2$,
Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(c) with $n=1$ and
$\beta_r(1)=1/\Phi_r(1)=r-1$; for $r\le2$, Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(b)
with $S=[1,L-1]$, where $q=r_S$ is forced and $\mu_r(S,L)=a^L$.
\end{proof}

At $r=2$ both values are $1$.  For the other rows at $r\ge2$ we need the
hole integral at the root $r$.  For finite nonempty $C\subset\N_{>0}$ with
$m=\max C$ and $w_c$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint}, put
\[
  T_C(u)=\sum_{c\in C}w_cr^{-c}u^{m-c}(1+u)^{c-1},\qquad
  H_C(t)=\sum_{c\in C}|w_c|\,r^{-c}t^{m-c}(1-t)^{c-1}.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Hole integral at the root $r$]\label{lem:holeintr}
Let $r>1$, $k\ge0$ and $P=[1,m+k]\setminus C$.  Then
\[
  \Phi_r(P)=\int_0^1t^k\bigl(a^{k+1}T_C(at)+H_C(t)\bigr)\,dt,
\]
where the second summand integrates to $\sum_{c\in C}|r_P(c)|r^{-c}$ and
the first to $\sum_{s>m+k}|r_P(s)|r^{-s}$.  In particular, for $c\ge1$ and
$t_0\ge0$,
\[
  S_r(c,t_0):=\Phi_r\bigl([1,c+t_0]\setminus\{c\}\bigr)
  =\frac{r^{-c}}{\binom{c+t_0}c}
   +c\,r^{-c}\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}\binom{c-1}i\frac{a^{t_0+i+1}}{t_0+i+1},
\]
with $S_r(1,t_0)=\psi_r(t_0+1)$ and $S_r(c,0)=a^c$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $x=m+k$.  As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint}, with
$\sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}xy^s=(y/(1-y))^{x+1}$ at $y=u/r$ and then
$v=u/(r-u)$,
\[
  \sum_{s>x}\binom{s-1}x\frac{r^{-s}}{s-c}
  =\int_0^1\frac{u^{x-c}\,du}{(r-u)^{x+1}}
  =r^{-c}\int_0^av^{x-c}(1+v)^{c-1}\,dv
  =r^{-c}a^{x-c+1}\int_0^1t^{x-c}(1+at)^{c-1}\,dt,
\]
and $a^{x-c+1}t^{x-c}=a^{k+1}t^k(at)^{m-c}$.  The values at the holes are
$|r_P(c)|=|w_c|\int_0^1t^{x-c}(1-t)^{c-1}dt$ exactly as there.  For
$C=\{c\}$, $w_c=c$, the hole value is $r^{-c}/\binom xc$, and expanding
$(1+v)^{c-1}$ gives the sum.  For $c=1$ it is $(1+a^{t_0+1})/(r(t_0+1))$;
for $t_0=0$ the set is $[1,c-1]$.
\end{proof}

At $r=2$, $a=1$ and $T_C+H_C=M_C$, which is Lemma~\ref{lem:holeint}.  For
$r>2$ the tail carries the extra factor $a^{k+1}$; the target $\psi_r$
splits accordingly as $\frac1{r(k+1)}+\frac{a^{k+1}}{r(k+1)}$.

\begin{lemma}[A sufficient condition]\label{lem:psiprin}
Let $r\ge2$, $k\ge0$ and $G_C(t)=r\bigl(H_C(t)+T_C(at)\bigr)$.  If
\[
  \int_0^1t^kG_C(t)\,dt\le\frac2{k+1}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \int_0^1t^k\,rH_C(t)\,dt\le\frac1{k+1},
\]
then $\Phi_r([1,m+k]\setminus C)\le\psi_r(k+1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:holeintr},
$r\Phi_r=\int_0^1t^k\bigl(a^{k+1}G_C+(1-a^{k+1})rH_C\bigr)dt$, and
$0<a\le1$, so $r\Phi_r\le\frac{2a^{k+1}+1-a^{k+1}}{k+1}=r\psi_r(k+1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rows with the target $\psi_r$]\label{prop:psirows}
Let $r\ge2$, $n\ge1$ and $A\subset\N_{>0}$ finite.  Some finite $Z\supseteq A$
has $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$ and $\Phi_r(Z)\le\psi_r(n)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n=1$ take $Z=A$: $\Phi_r(A)\le\Phi_r(\varnothing)=a=\psi_r(1)$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr}.  Let $n\ge2$, let $j$ be the leading run of $A$,
and let $c_1=j+1<c_2<\cdots$ be the positive integers outside $A$.  Keep the
holes $K=\{1\}$, $\{2\}$, $\{3,c_2\}$, $\{c_1,\ldots,c_{j+1}\}$ for $j=0$,
$1$, $2$, $j\ge3$, fill the next $n-1$ holes, and let $x$ be the last filled
hole, $P=[1,x]\setminus K$ and $Z=P\cup A$, exactly as in
Propositions~\ref{prop:rowsfree} and~\ref{prop:rowstop}.  Then $Z\supseteq A$,
$|Z|\le|A|+n-1$, and $\Phi_r(Z)\le\Phi_r(P)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr},
the elements of $Z$ above $x$ being deleted one at a time.  Put
$m=\max K$ and $k=x-m\ge n-1$.  Since $\psi_r$ is decreasing ($a\le1$), it
suffices to show $\Phi_r(P)\le\psi_r(k+1)$, or, for $j\ge3$,
$\Phi_r(P)\le(1+a^n)/(r(k+1))$.

\emph{$j=0$.}  $P=[2,x]$ and $\Phi_r(P)=\psi_r(x)=\psi_r(k+1)$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:holeintr}.

\emph{$j=1$.}  $C=\{2\}$: $rH_C=\frac2r(1-t)\le1$ and
$G_C=\frac2r\bigl(2-(1-a)t\bigr)\le\frac4r\le2$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:psiprin} applies.

\emph{$j=2$, $b=c_2\ge5$.}  $C=\{3,b\}$, $w_b=-w_3=\kappa:=3b/(b-3)$.  Writing
$B_i=\binom{b-1}it^i(1-t)^{b-1-i}$ and $1+at=(1-t)+rat$, a direct expansion gives
$G_C=\kappa\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\gamma_iB_i$ with
\[
  \gamma_0=2r^{1-b},\quad
  \gamma_i=r^{1-b}(ra)^i\ (1\le i\le b-4),\quad
  \gamma_{b-3}=\frac{a^{b-3}(1-\epsilon)+\epsilon}{r^2},\quad
  \gamma_{b-2}=\frac{a^{b-2}}r\cdot\frac{b-3}{b-1},\quad
  \gamma_{b-1}=0,
\]
where $\epsilon=1/\binom{b-1}2$.  With $r\ge2$, $a\le1$ and $ra\le2$:
$\gamma_0\le2^{2-b}\le\frac18$ and $\gamma_i\le2^{1-b+i}\le\frac18$ for $1\le i\le b-4$, so $\kappa\gamma_i\le\frac{3b}{8(b-3)}\le1$ for $i\le b-4$;
$\kappa\gamma_{b-3}\le\kappa/4\le\frac{15}8$ and
$\kappa\gamma_{b-2}\le\frac{3b}{2(b-1)}\le2$.  So $G_C\le2$.  Next
$rH_C=\kappa\bigl(r^{-2}t^{b-3}(1-t)^2+r^{1-b}(1-t)^{b-1}\bigr)$, and
$\int_0^1t^kt^{b-3}(1-t)^2\,dt\le\frac2{(k+1)b(b-1)}$,
$\int_0^1t^k(1-t)^{b-1}dt\le\frac1{k+1}$, so
$(k+1)\int t^k rH_C\le\frac3{2(b-3)(b-1)}+\frac{3b\,2^{1-b}}{b-3}\le\frac3{16}+\frac{15}{32}<1$.

\emph{$j=2$, $b=4$.}  Here $T_C(at)=12r^{-4}(1+at)^2(1-t)$ because
$1+at-rat=1-t$, so
\[
  G_C=12(1-t)\Bigl(\frac{t(1-t)}{r^2}+\frac{(1-t)^2}{r^3}
       +\frac1r\Bigl(\frac{1+at}r\Bigr)^2\Bigr)\le3(1-t^2),
\]
each term being largest at $r=2$ ($(1+at)/r$ decreases in $r$), and
$\int_0^1t^k3(1-t^2)\,dt=\frac6{(k+1)(k+3)}\le\frac2{k+1}$.  Also
$rH_C\le3t(1-t)^2+\frac32(1-t)^3$, whose moment is
$\frac{6k+15}{(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)(k+4)}\le\frac1{k+1}$.

\emph{$j\ge3$.}  Here $k\ge1$.  Split
$\Phi_r(P)=H_r+T_r$ into the values at the kept holes and the tail, as in
the paragraph before Lemma~\ref{lem:holevals}.  Every kept hole is at least
$j+1$ and $2/r\le1$, so with $H(C,k)$ of that paragraph and
Lemma~\ref{lem:holevals},
\[
  H_r\le(2/r)^{j+1}H(C,k)\le\frac{(2/r)^{j+1}}{2(k+1)}\le\frac1{r(k+1)} .
\]
For the tail, the first two steps of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:holetail}
do not involve the root, and summing
$\binom{s-1}xr^{-s}(1-t)^{s-m-1}$ over $s>x$ gives
$(1-t)^k(r-1+t)^{-x-1}=a^{x+1}(1-t)^k(1+at)^{-x-1}$, so
\[
  T_r\le(j+1)\binom mj a^{m+k+1}\int_0^1t^j
     \Bigl(\frac{1-t}{1+at}\Bigr)^k(1+at)^{-m-1}\,dt .
\]
The function $\log(1+at)-\log(1-t)$ vanishes at $0$, has derivative $1+a$
there and is convex (as $a/(1+at)\le1\le1/(1-t)$), so
$(1-t)/(1+at)\le e^{-(1+a)t}$; and $(k+1)e^{-(1+a)kt}\le2/(e(1+a)t)$.
Extending the integral to $(0,\infty)$ and using
$\int_0^\infty t^{j-1}(1+at)^{-m-1}dt=a^{-j}\mathrm B(j,m-j+1)$,
$\binom mj\mathrm B(j,m-j+1)=1/j$ and $1+a=ra$,
\[
  (k+1)T_r\le\frac{2(j+1)}{e\,j\,r}\,a^{m+k-j}
  \le\frac{2(j+1)}{e\,j}\,a^{j}\cdot\frac{a^n}r\le\frac{a^n}r,
\]
because $m\ge2j+1$ and $k+1\ge n$ give $m+k-j\ge n+j$, and
$2(j+1)/(ej)\le8/(3e)<1$ for $j\ge3$.  Hence
$\Phi_r(P)\le(1+a^n)/(r(k+1))\le\psi_r(n)$.
\end{proof}

At $r=2$ the proposition is $T(n)$.  For larger $r$ its target $\psi_r(n)$
is weaker than the row $a^n$ of \eqref{eq:order} once $(r-1)^n+1>nr$; the
remedy is to keep one hole in the middle.

\begin{lemma}[Moving the hole]\label{lem:shift}
For $r\ge2$ and $t_0\ge0$, $S_r(2,t_0)\le S_r(1,t_0)$.  For $r\ge\frac52$,
$h\ge1$ and $t_0\ge0$, $S_r(h+1,t_0)\le S_r(h,t_0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:holeintr},
$S_r(2,t_0)=\frac2{r^2}\bigl(\frac{a^{t_0+1}}{t_0+1}+\frac{a^{t_0+2}}{t_0+2}
+\frac1{(t_0+1)(t_0+2)}\bigr)$, so
$r(t_0+1)S_r(2,t_0)=\frac2r\bigl(a^{t_0+1}+\frac{(t_0+1)a^{t_0+2}+1}{t_0+2}\bigr)
\le\frac2r(1+a^{t_0+1})\le1+a^{t_0+1}=r(t_0+1)S_r(1,t_0)$.
For $h\ge2$ write $S_r(h,t_0)=\eta(h)+\tau(h)$ with
$\eta(h)=r^{-h}/\binom{h+t_0}h$ and
$\tau(h)=hr^{-h}\int_0^av^{t_0}(1+v)^{h-1}dv$.  Then
$\eta(h+1)=\frac{h+1}{r(h+1+t_0)}\eta(h)\le\eta(h)$, and $1+v\le1+a=ra$
gives $\tau(h+1)\le\frac{h+1}h\,a\,\tau(h)\le\frac32a\,\tau(h)\le\tau(h)$
for $r\ge\frac52$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rows with one kept hole]\label{prop:oneholerows}
Let $r\ge2$, $1\le c\le n$ and $A\subset\N_{>0}$ finite.  Some finite
$Z\supseteq A$ has $|Z|\le|A|+n-1$ and
$\Phi_r(Z)\le\sup_{h\ge c}S_r(h,n-c)$.  The supremum is $S_r(c,n-c)$ when
$r\ge\frac52$, and also when $c=n$, where it is $a^n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $c_1<c_2<\cdots$ be the positive integers outside $A$, keep the hole
$h=c_c\ge c$, fill the holes $c_i$, $i\le n$, $i\ne c$, and put $x=c_n$ and
$Z=([1,x]\setminus\{h\})\cup A$.  Then $|Z\setminus A|=n-1$, and deleting the
elements of $Z$ above $h+n-c\le x$ one at a time (Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr})
gives $\Phi_r(Z)\le S_r(h,n-c)$.  For $r\ge\frac52$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:shift} applied $h-c$ times gives $S_r(h,n-c)\le
S_r(c,n-c)$; for $c=n$, $S_r(h,0)=a^h\le a^n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Every row at the root $r$]\label{thm:everyrowr}
Let $r\ge2$, $L\ge1$, let $F$ be a monic multiple of $(x-r)^L$, $1\le k\le L$
and $n=L-k+1$.  Then
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ \max\Bigl\{(r-1)^n,\ \frac{nr(r-1)^n}{(r-1)^n+1}\Bigr\},
\]
and if $r\ge\frac52$,
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ B_r(n):=\max_{1\le c\le n}\frac1{S_r(c,n-c)},\qquad
  S_r(c,n-c)=\frac{r^{-c}}{\binom nc}
   +c\,r^{-c}\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}\binom{c-1}i\frac{(r-1)^{-(n-c+i+1)}}{n-c+i+1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be as in Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(a), $|S|=k-1$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:psirows} with $A=S$ gives $Z\supseteq S$,
$|Z|\le L-1$, $\Phi_r(Z)\le\psi_r(n)$, and $1/\psi_r(n)$ is the second
term; the first is \eqref{eq:order} (or Proposition~\ref{prop:oneholerows}
with $c=n$).  For $r\ge\frac52$ use Proposition~\ref{prop:oneholerows} for
each $c$.  In each case Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(a) concludes.
\end{proof}

At $r=2$ the first bound is $\max\{1,n\}=L-k+1$, which is
Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow}.  The terms $c=1$ and $c=n$ of $B_r(n)$ are the
two terms of the first bound, so $B_r(n)$ dominates it.  Both rows at the
bottom are exact.

\begin{corollary}[The last two rows]\label{cor:tworowsr}
Let $r\ge2$ and $L\ge2$.  Then $V_r(L,L)=r-1$ and
\[
  V_r(L,L-1)=(r-1)^2\max\Bigl\{1,\frac{2r}{r^2-2r+2}\Bigr\},
\]
the maximum being the second term exactly when $r\le2+\sqrt2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first is Corollary~\ref{cor:lastrowr}.  For $n=2$ the lower bound is
Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrowr}, as $(r-1)^2+1=r^2-2r+2$.  For the upper bound,
$V_r(L,L-1)\le\beta_r(2)$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(c), and
$\mu_r(\varnothing,2)$ is the minimum over $\omega\in\R$ of
$\sum_s|1-\omega s|r^{-s}$, a convex function of $\omega$ that is linear
between consecutive points $1/s$, exceeds its value $a$ at $\omega=0$ for
$\omega<0$, and tends to infinity; so the minimum is $a$ or some
$\Phi_r(\{z\})$.  Summing directly,
\[
  \Phi_r(\{z\})=\frac{z(r-1)-r+2r^{1-z}}{z(r-1)^2}=a-\frac{a^2}z\,g(z),\qquad
  g(z)=r-2r^{1-z}.
\]
Now $g(1)=r-2$, $g(2)/2-g(3)/3=(r-2)(r^2+2r-2)/(6r^2)\ge0$, and for $z\ge4$,
$g(z)/z<r/4\le r/2-1/r=g(2)/2$.  So the minimum is $\min\{\Phi_r(\{1\}),
\Phi_r(\{2\})\}=\min\{a^2,(r^2-2r+2)a^2/(2r)\}$, which is at most $a$, and
$\beta_r(2)$ is the stated value.  The comparison $2r\ge r^2-2r+2$ is
$r^2-4r+2\le0$.
\end{proof}

At $r=2$ the corollary says $V_2(L,L-1)=2$: Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow} is
sharp in its two lowest rows.  The threshold $2+\sqrt2$ is where the
criterion quoted after Proposition~\ref{prop:nonsharp} starts to hold for
$P_{L-2}=(x-r)^2$: its first partial sum $1-2r$ has absolute value at most
$(r-1)^2$ exactly when $r^2-4r+2\ge0$.

\paragraph{How far the bound is from the rows.}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}, $B_r(n)\le V_r(L,k)\le\beta_r(n)$ for
$r\ge\frac52$, and $\beta_r(n)$ is the top row of $(x-r)^n$.  We computed
$1/\Phi_r(Z_0)$ exactly for the best zero set $Z_0$ of size $n-1$ inside
$[1,\min(3n+3,18)]$; this is a lower bound for $\beta_r(n)$, and presumably
its value.  It equals $B_r(n)$ for $r=6$, $n\le7$; $r=4$, $n\le5$; $r=3$,
$n\le3$; $r=\frac52$, $n\le2$.  For example $B_3(3)=\frac{27}2$ at $c=2$ and
$B_4(4)=128$ at $c=3$.  Beyond that the two separate, for instance
$B_3(5)=80$ against $\frac{31104}{307}=101.3\ldots$ and $B_3(7)=407.2\ldots$
against $815.8\ldots$; in the computed range the ratio decreases with $n$.
At $r=2$ the computed values are $1,2,\frac{40}{11},\frac{672}{115},
\frac{10368}{851}$ for $n\le5$, against $n$.  The one-hole family is not
optimal in general: the best zero sets of the top row, such as
$\{1,3,5\}$ at $(r,n)=(3,4)$, spread their gaps.  Numerically
Lemma~\ref{lem:shift} holds for all $h,t_0<50$ at $r=2.3$ and fails at
$r=2.2$ ($h=3$, $t_0=13$), so the constant $\frac52$ is not the limit of
the method.

\paragraph{Rows equal the top row.}
For $r\ge2$ we conjecture $V_r(L,k)=\beta_r(L-k+1)$: exempting $k-1$
positions exactly offsets $k-1$ of the $L$ zeros at $r$.  In the form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(b), this says that every finite $S$ admits
$Z\supseteq S$ with $|Z|\le|S|+n-1$ and $\Phi_r(Z)\le\mu_r(\varnothing,n)$.
It holds for $n=1$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr}, and for $n=2$ by
Corollary~\ref{cor:tworowsr}.  For $r\in\{2,\frac94,\frac52,3,4\}$,
$n\le4$ and every $S\subseteq[1,9]$ with $|S|\le3$ we found such a $Z$,
with $\mu_r(\varnothing,n)$ replaced by $\Phi_r(Z_0)$ for the $Z_0$ of the
previous paragraph and the inequality checked in exact arithmetic.  The
worst $S$ found always lies at the top of the search range, as the limit in
Proposition~\ref{prop:rowvalue}(c) suggests.  At $r=2$ the conjecture would
sharpen Theorem~\ref{thm:everyrow} to $b_k\ge\beta_2(L-k+1)$, with
$\beta_2(3)\ge\frac{40}{11}>3$.  It fails for $r<2$: there
$V_r(L,L)=(r-1)^L<r-1=\beta_r(1)$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:lastrowr}.

\paragraph{Roots between one and two.}
For $1<r<2$ the deletion of Lemma~\ref{lem:confdelr} can increase $\Phi_r$
by the factor $a>1$, and the exempted set $S=[1,k-1]$ is costly: every
$Z\supseteq[1,k-1]$ has
$\Phi_r(Z)=a^k\sum_{s\ge k}\binom{s-1}{k-1}(1-r^{-1})^kr^{-(s-k)}|q(s)|$
with $q=r_{Z\setminus[1,k-1]}$, a negative-binomial mean inflated by $a^k$.
What is proved here is the top row, $V_r(L,1)=\beta_r(L)\ge\max_c1/S_r(c,L-c)$
(any certificate is admissible when $S=\varnothing$), and the last row,
$V_r(L,L)=(r-1)^L$; Corollary~\ref{cor:ordergen} gives only $(r-1)^L$ for
the rows in between.  In a numerical search at $r=\frac32$ and $\frac74$
with $L\le5$, $S\subseteq[1,8]$ and zero sets in a window, the worst
exempted set is $S=[1,k-1]$ in all but one case, and the approximate row
values at $r=\frac32$, $L=5$ are $2.98,\,1.99,\,0.80,\,0.20,\,\frac1{32}$
for $k=1,\ldots,5$ (the last is exact).  We have not determined these
rows.

\section{Complex roots}\label{sec:complexroots}

Everything so far prescribes real roots.  This section shows that complex
roots split into two regimes.  Roots that come in full orbits of
$x\mapsto\zeta x$, $\zeta^m=1$, over real values of $x^m$, in particular
purely imaginary conjugate pairs $\pm ic$, reduce \emph{exactly} to real
roots: the infimum of every order statistic and of $\Lambda$ over monic
multiples is the same as for the real roots $c^2$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}, Corollary~\ref{cor:imag}).  Without such
structure nothing beyond the first row survives: the first row holds at
every root set (Proposition~\ref{prop:rowone}), but the rows $k\ge2$ and every
mass bound of more than linear order fail for pairs lying within any fixed
angle of the imaginary axis (Proposition~\ref{prop:noangle}).

\begin{lemma}[Multisection]\label{lem:multisection}
Let $m\ge1$, let $R\in\mathbb C[y]$ with $R(0)\ne0$, and put $P(x)=R(x^m)$.  Write
a polynomial $F\in\mathbb C[x]$ as
\[
  F(x)=\sum_{\varepsilon=0}^{m-1}x^\varepsilon E_\varepsilon(x^m),
  \qquad E_\varepsilon(y)=\sum_{i\ge0}f_{mi+\varepsilon}\,y^i .
\]
If $P$ divides $F$, then $R$ divides every $E_\varepsilon$.  If $F$ and $R$
are real, so are the $E_\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/m}$.  Since $P(\zeta^kx)=R(\zeta^{km}x^m)=P(x)$, the
polynomial $P$ divides $F(\zeta^kx)$ for every $k$, hence divides
\[
  \frac1m\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{-k\varepsilon}F(\zeta^kx)
  =x^\varepsilon E_\varepsilon(x^m).
\]
As $R(0)\ne0$, $P(0)\ne0$, so $x^\varepsilon$ is coprime to $P$ and
$R(x^m)\mid E_\varepsilon(x^m)$.  Divide in $\mathbb C[y]$:
$E_\varepsilon=RQ+S$ with $\deg S<\deg R$.  Then $R(x^m)$ divides
$S(x^m)$, whose degree $m\deg S$ is smaller than $m\deg R$, so $S=0$.
Reality of $E_\varepsilon$ is clear from its coefficients.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Multisection transfer]\label{thm:transfer}
Let $m$, $R$ and $P=R(x^m)$ be as in Lemma~\ref{lem:multisection}, and let
$F$ be a nonzero multiple of $P$ of degree $D$.  For the residue
$\varepsilon\equiv D\pmod m$, the polynomial $E_\varepsilon$ is a multiple of
$R$ of degree $(D-\varepsilon)/m$ with leading coefficient $f_D$, and
\[
  b_k(F)\ge b_k(E_\varepsilon)\quad(k\ge1),\qquad
  \Lambda(F)\ge\Lambda(E_\varepsilon).
\]
Conversely, for every multiple $G$ of $R$ the polynomial $G(x^m)$ is a
multiple of $P$ with the same nonzero coefficients.  Consequently, for
$\Phi=b_k$ (any fixed $k$) and for $\Phi=\Lambda$,
\[
  \inf\{\Phi(F):F\text{ monic},\ P\mid F\}
  =\inf\{\Phi(G):G\text{ monic},\ R\mid G\},
\]
over complex multiples, and also over real multiples when $R$ is real.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:multisection}, $R\mid E_\varepsilon$.  The top coefficient
of $F$ sits at $D=m\cdot\frac{D-\varepsilon}m+\varepsilon$, so it is the
coefficient of $y^{(D-\varepsilon)/m}$ in $E_\varepsilon$, and no higher
coefficient of $E_\varepsilon$ is nonzero.  The nonleading coefficients of
$E_\varepsilon$ are the $f_j$ with $j\equiv\varepsilon$, $j<D$: a
sub-multiset of the nonleading coefficients of $F$.  The $k$-th largest
element of a sub-multiset is at most the $k$-th largest of the whole, and
$\Lambda$ is a sum of nonnegative terms over coefficients; this gives the
two inequalities.  Monicity passes from $F$ to $E_\varepsilon$, which gives
$\ge$ between the infima.  For the converse, $R\mid G$ gives
$R(x^m)\mid G(x^m)$, and $G(x^m)$ has the coefficients of $G$ spread out
with zeros between them, so $b_k$ and $\Lambda$ are unchanged, and
$G(x^m)$ is monic when $G$ is.  This gives $\le$.
\end{proof}

The case $m=2$, $R(y)=\prod_j(y+c_j^2)$ is the case of purely imaginary
conjugate pairs; the sign change $y\mapsto-y$ turns $R$ into a product of
linear factors at the positive roots $c_j^2$ without changing any
coefficient magnitude.

\begin{corollary}[Purely imaginary pairs]\label{cor:imag}
Let $c_1\le\cdots\le c_K$ be positive reals, repetitions allowed, put
$P_{\rm im}=\prod_{j=1}^K(x^2+c_j^2)$ and
$P_{\rm re}=\prod_{j=1}^K(y-c_j^2)$, and let $F\in\mathbb C[x]$ be a nonzero
multiple of $P_{\rm im}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $c_1^2\ge2$, then for $1\le k\le K$,
\[
  b_k(F)\ \ge\ |f_D|\prod_{j=k}^K(c_j^2-1),
\]
and the $k$ magnitudes may be taken among the positions $j\equiv D\pmod2$.
The row $k=1$ holds for $c_1>1$.
\item If $c_1^2\ge2$ and $|f_D|\ge1$, then
\[
  \Lambda(F)\ \ge\ (K+1)\log|f_D|+\sum_{j=1}^Kj\log(c_j^2-1).
\]
\item For $\Phi=b_k$ and $\Phi=\Lambda$, the infimum of $\Phi$ over monic
(real or complex) multiples of $P_{\rm im}$ equals its infimum over monic
multiples of $P_{\rm re}$.  In particular, with $c_1^2\ge2$ and $K\ge2$,
\[
  \inf_{Q\text{ monic}}\Lambda(P_{\rm im}Q)\ \asymp\
  K^2+\sum_{j=1}^Kj\log(c_j^2-1)
\]
with the absolute constants of Corollary~\ref{cor:allroots};
$\Lambda(F)\ge K^2/80$ for monic $F$ when $K\ge8$; and the row $k=2$ is an
infimum at $(c_1^2,c_2^2)=(2,3)$, the rows $k=2,3$ at
$(c_1^2,c_2^2,c_3^2)=(2,3,5)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} with $m=2$ and $R(y)=\prod_j(y+c_j^2)$;
$R(0)\ne0$.  The multiple $E_\varepsilon$ of $R$ has leading coefficient
$f_D$, and $\tilde E(y)=E_\varepsilon(-y)$ is a multiple of $P_{\rm re}$
with leading coefficient $\pm f_D$ and the same coefficient magnitudes.  For
real $F$, Theorem~\ref{thm:order} (with Corollary~\ref{cor:complex} for complex $F$) applied to
$\tilde E$ at the roots $c_j^2\ge2$ gives item~1, since
$b_k(F)\ge b_k(\tilde E)$ and the positions of $\tilde E$ are those
$j\equiv D\pmod 2$ of $F$; the clause $c_1>1$ is the $u=0$ clause of
Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  Item~2 is Corollary~\ref{cor:mass} applied to $\tilde E$, together with
$\Lambda(F)\ge\Lambda(\tilde E)$.  Item~3 is the equality of infima in
Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer}, composed with the magnitude-preserving
bijection $G(y)\mapsto\pm G(-y)$ between monic multiples of $R$ and of
$P_{\rm re}$; the three quoted statements are Corollary~\ref{cor:allroots},
Proposition~\ref{prop:quadratic} and Propositions~\ref{prop:sharp23} and~\ref{prop:sharp235} of the real-root theory at
the roots $c_j^2$.
\end{proof}

Thus a purely imaginary pair $\pm ic$ is worth exactly one real root $c^2$:
the pair has two roots of modulus $c$, and the mass charges $\log c^2$ once
at its rank.  This is the right count.  By item~3 the product
$P_{\rm im}$ itself is within $O(K^2)$ of the infimum
(Corollary~\ref{cor:near}), and $\Lambda(P_{\rm im})=\Lambda(P_{\rm re})$; so a
bound of the form $\sum_{i\le2K}i\log\rho_{(i)}$ over the $2K$ roots
counted separately, which would be about twice as large, is false already
for $P_{\rm im}$.

The same proof applies to every factor of the form $R(x^m)$ with $R$ real
and all roots of $R$ of one sign: the real roots $\pm r$ in pairs
($R=\prod(y-r_j^2)$), or the $m$-th roots of $\pm s_j$ for fixed $m$.  One
more reduction needs no multisection.

\begin{corollary}[Roots on a common ray]\label{cor:ray}
Let $\theta\in\R$ and $\rho_1\le\cdots\le\rho_K$ with $\rho_1\ge2$, and let
$F\in\mathbb C[x]$ be a nonzero multiple of $\prod_j(x-\rho_je^{i\theta})$.  Then
$b_k(F)\ge|f_D|\prod_{j\ge k}(\rho_j-1)$ for $1\le k\le K$.  In
particular this holds for a real $F$ divisible by
$\prod_j(x^2-2\rho_j\cos\theta\,x+\rho_j^2)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$G(x)=F(e^{i\theta}x)$ has coefficients $f_je^{ij\theta}$, of the same
magnitudes, and is divisible by $\prod_j(x-\rho_j)$; apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:complex}.
\end{proof}

A common ray charges each pair once, with $\log(\rho-1)$ in place of
$\log(\rho^2-1)$; the imaginary axis is special because it carries both
members of each pair.

\begin{proposition}[The first row at arbitrary roots]\label{prop:rowone}
Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_N\in\mathbb C$, repetitions allowed, with
$\rho_j=|\alpha_j|$, and let $F\in\mathbb C[x]$ be a nonzero multiple of
$\prod_j(x-\alpha_j)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If every $\rho_j>1$, then $b_1(F)\ge|f_D|\prod_{j=1}^N(\rho_j-1)$.
\item For every $0<r<1$,
$b_1(F)\ge|f_D|\,\frac{1-r}{r}\bigl(r^N\mathcal M-1\bigr)$, where
$\mathcal M=\prod_j\max(1,\rho_j)$; with $r=N/(N+1)$,
$b_1(F)\ge|f_D|\,(\mathcal M/e-1)/N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $F^*(t)=t^DF(1/t)=\sum_{d=0}^Df_{D-d}t^d$ and $\beta_j=1/\alpha_j$
where $\alpha_j\ne0$.

\emph{Item 1.}  Here every $\alpha_j\ne0$, and if $(x-\alpha)^\mu\mid F$
then $F^*(t)=(1-\alpha t)^\mu\,t^{D-\mu}H(1/t)$ with $F=(x-\alpha)^\mu H$, so
$\chi(t)=\prod_j(t-\beta_j)$ divides $F^*$.  Put
$B(s)=\prod_j(1-\beta_js)=s^N\chi(1/s)$, let $c=-[s^N]B$, and let
$v_d=[s^d]V$ for $V=(B+cs^N)/B=1+cs^N/B$.  The numerator $A=B+cs^N$ has
degree at most $N-1$, so $BV=A$ gives, for every $n\ge N$,
$\sum_{k=0}^N[s^k]B\;v_{n-k}=0$, that is,
$\sum_{i=0}^N\chi_iv_{m'+i}=0$ for every $m'\ge0$, where
$\chi=\sum_i\chi_it^i$.  Writing $F^*=\chi h$,
\[
  \sum_{d}v_df_{D-d}
  =\sum_{m'}h_{m'}\sum_i\chi_iv_{m'+i}=0 .
\]
Since $v_0=1$, $|f_D|\le b_1(F)\sum_{d\ge1}|v_d|$.  Now
$|c|=\prod_j|\beta_j|=\prod_j\rho_j^{-1}$, and the coefficients of $1/B$ are
dominated in modulus by those of $\prod_j(1-|\beta_j|s)^{-1}$, whose sum is
$\prod_j(1-\rho_j^{-1})^{-1}$.  Hence
$\sum_{d\ge1}|v_d|\le\prod_j\rho_j^{-1}(1-\rho_j^{-1})^{-1}
=\prod_j(\rho_j-1)^{-1}$.

\emph{Item 2.}  $F^*(0)=f_D\ne0$, and $F^*$ vanishes at every $\beta_j$
(counted with multiplicity, as in item 1).  Jensen's formula on $|t|\le r$
gives
\[
  \log\max_{|t|=r}|F^*|\ \ge\ \log|f_D|+\sum_{j:\rho_j>1/r}\log(r\rho_j)
  \ \ge\ \log|f_D|+\sum_{j}\log\bigl(r\max(1,\rho_j)\bigr),
\]
since each omitted or added term is $\le0$ on the right.  On the other side
$\max_{|t|=r}|F^*|\le|f_D|+b_1(F)\sum_{d\ge1}r^d
=|f_D|+b_1(F)\,r/(1-r)$.  Rearranging gives the first bound; for
$r=N/(N+1)$, $(1-r)/r=1/N$ and $r^N=(1+1/N)^{-N}\ge e^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

For real roots $r_j>1$ item 1 is the row $k=1$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:order}.  Both items
are of the size of the Mahler measure: they charge each root once, as
$\log\rho_j$, and do not give a second large coefficient.  At arbitrary
angles nothing more is true.

\begin{proposition}[No angle-uniform rows beyond the first]
\label{prop:noangle}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $M\ge3$ be odd, $\rho>1$, and $0<\delta<\pi/2$.  The monic
polynomial $x^{2M}+\rho^{2M}$ is divisible by the product of the
$K=2\lfloor\delta M/\pi\rfloor+1$ conjugate pairs of modulus $\rho$ with
arguments $\pm(\frac\pi2+t\frac\pi M)$, $|t|\le\delta M/\pi$, all within
$\delta$ of the imaginary axis; yet
\[
  b_2(x^{2M}+\rho^{2M})=0,\qquad
  \Lambda(x^{2M}+\rho^{2M})=2M\log\rho\le\frac\pi\delta(K+1)\log\rho .
\]
\item For $m\ge2$ and $B\ge1$, the polynomial $x^{2m}+x+B$ has no real
root, so its roots form $m$ conjugate pairs, and $b_2=1$,
$\Lambda=\log B$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, no bound $b_2(F)\ge\phi(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_K)>0$ in terms of
the moduli of the prescribed pairs alone, not even for pairs within a fixed
angle $\delta$ of the imaginary axis, and no bound
$\Lambda(F)\ge c\,K^2\log\rho$ for $K$ pairs of modulus $\rho$ within a
fixed angle $\delta$, can hold for all monic multiples.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
1.  The roots of $x^{2M}+\rho^{2M}$ are $\rho e^{i\pi(2l+1)/(2M)}$; for
$M$ odd, $l=(M-1)/2+t$ gives the argument $\frac\pi2+t\frac\pi M$, and the
conjugate root has the negated argument.  The integers $t$ with
$|t|\le\delta M/\pi$ number $K$.  The only nonleading nonzero coefficient
is $\rho^{2M}$, so $b_2=0$ and $\Lambda=2M\log\rho$.  Finally
$K\ge2\delta M/\pi-1$ gives $2M\le\pi(K+1)/\delta$.

2.  For real $x$ with $|x|\ge1$, $x^{2m}+x\ge|x|^{2m}-|x|\ge0$, and for
$|x|<1$, $x^{2m}+x>-1$; so $x^{2m}+x+B>0$ when $B\ge1$.  The coefficients are
$1,1,B$.

For the final sentence, item 1 with $\delta M\ge\pi$, so $K\ge3$, has $b_2=0$ while every
$\rho_j=\rho$ is fixed, and letting $M\to\infty$ at fixed $\delta$ gives
$K\to\infty$ with $\Lambda=O_\delta(K\log\rho)$.
\end{proof}

Item 2 has distinct moduli: numerically $(2.1477,2.1545,2.1611)$ at
$(m,B)=(3,100)$.  So distinct moduli do not help either; what the
imaginary axis provides in Corollary~\ref{cor:imag} is not closeness of the
angles but exact membership, which makes $R(x^2)$ a polynomial in $x^2$.

\subsection{Gaussian-integer roots}\label{sec:gaussian}

Proposition~\ref{prop:noangle} rules out bounds in terms of moduli and
angles.  For roots in $\mathbb Z[i]$ and integer multiples there is one more
piece of information: an integer polynomial takes Gaussian-integer values at
them, and a nonzero Gaussian integer has modulus at least one.  This
subsection shows what that buys.  It does not give the rows $k\ge2$ at
Gaussian pairs, but it reduces the mass problem to multiples of bounded
degree, which makes it a finite computation for each root set
(Corollary~\ref{cor:gaussfinite}), and it certifies the imaginary-pair
charge $\sum_jj\log(c_j^2-1)$ at explicit root sets with $a_j\ne0$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussinst}).  Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussnotransfer}
records why the exact reductions of this section cannot be used at such
roots.

Throughout, $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K\in\mathbb Z[i]$ are distinct, with
$\alpha_j=a_j+ic_j$ and $c_j\ge1$; put $\rho_j=|\alpha_j|$,
$\rho_{\min}=\min_j\rho_j$ and
\[
  P=\prod_{j=1}^K(x-\alpha_j)(x-\bar\alpha_j)
   =\prod_{j=1}^K\bigl(x^2-2a_jx+a_j^2+c_j^2\bigr)\in\mathbb Z[x],
\]
a monic polynomial with $2K$ distinct roots and $P(0)\ne0$.  For
$F=\sum_{i=0}^Df_ix^i$ and $0\le m\le D+1$ write
\[
  R_m=\sum_{i<m}f_ix^i,\qquad G_m=\sum_{i\ge m}f_ix^{i-m},\qquad
  F=R_m+x^mG_m .
\]
We call $m\in\{1,\ldots,D\}$ a \emph{split} of $F$.  A split is \emph{clean
at} $\alpha$ if $G_m(\alpha)=0$, and \emph{$P$-clean} if it is clean at every
$\alpha_j$.  A polynomial $B\in\mathbb Z[x]$ is \emph{$P$-irreducible} if
$B(0)\ne0$ and no split of $B$ is $P$-clean.

\begin{lemma}[Gaussian carries]\label{lem:gausscarry}
Let $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ have degree $D$, let $\alpha\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with
$F(\alpha)=0$, $\rho=|\alpha|$, and let $1\le m\le D$.  Then
$G_m(\alpha)\in\mathbb Z[i]$ and $R_m(\alpha)=-\alpha^mG_m(\alpha)$.  If the
split $m$ is not clean at $\alpha$, then $|R_m(\alpha)|\ge\rho^m$, and if
moreover $\rho>2$, some $i<m$ has
\[
  |f_i|\ \ge\ (\rho/2)^{m-i}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$G_m$ has integer coefficients, so $G_m(\alpha)\in\mathbb Z[i]$, and
$0=F(\alpha)=R_m(\alpha)+\alpha^mG_m(\alpha)$.  If $G_m(\alpha)\ne0$ it is a
nonzero Gaussian integer, so $|G_m(\alpha)|\ge1$ and
$|R_m(\alpha)|=\rho^m|G_m(\alpha)|\ge\rho^m$.  If every $i<m$ had
$|f_i|<(\rho/2)^{m-i}$, then
$|R_m(\alpha)|\le\sum_{i<m}|f_i|\rho^i<\rho^m\sum_{i<m}2^{i-m}<\rho^m$, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

The lemma is the Gaussian form of the gap principle for lacunary
polynomials~\cite{lenstra-lacunary}: across a run of small coefficients a
root of $F$ must be a root of both parts.  Here it is used at every split,
not only at long gaps.

\begin{lemma}[Blocks]\label{lem:gaussblocks}
Every nonzero $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by $P$ can be written
\[
  F=\sum_{s=1}^rx^{e_s}B_s,\qquad e_s+\deg B_s<e_{s+1},
\]
with every $B_s\in\mathbb Z[x]$ $P$-irreducible and divisible by $P$.
Consequently $\Lambda(F)=\sum_s\Lambda(B_s)$, $|\operatorname{lead}B_s|\ge1$,
and $b_k(F)\ge b_k(B_s)$ for every $s$ and $k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $F=x^eF_0$ with $F_0(0)\ne0$; since $P(0)\ne0$, $P\mid F_0$.  Induct
on $\deg F_0$.  If no split of $F_0$ is $P$-clean, $F_0$ itself is the only
block.  Otherwise let $m$ be a $P$-clean split: $G_m(\alpha_j)=0$ for all
$j$, hence $R_m(\alpha_j)=F_0(\alpha_j)-\alpha_j^mG_m(\alpha_j)=0$, and by
reality of the coefficients also $R_m(\bar\alpha_j)=G_m(\bar\alpha_j)=0$.
Since $P$ is monic in $\mathbb Z[x]$ with $2K$ distinct roots, division with
remainder in $\mathbb Z[x]$ shows $P\mid R_m$ and $P\mid G_m$ in
$\mathbb Z[x]$.  Both are nonzero ($R_m(0)=F_0(0)$, and $G_m$ has the leading
coefficient of $F_0$) and of smaller degree than $F_0$; $R_m(0)\ne0$, and
$G_m$ is handled after removing a power of $x$.  Their supports lie in
$[0,m-1]$ and $[m,\deg F_0]$, so the decompositions given by induction
concatenate.  The supports of the $x^{e_s}B_s$ are disjoint and cover that of
$F$, which gives the additivity of $\Lambda$ and the inclusion of the
nonleading magnitudes of each $B_s$ among those of $F$ (the leading
coefficient of $B_s$, $s<r$, is a nonleading coefficient of $F$).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Degree charging]\label{thm:gausscharge}
Assume $\rho_{\min}>2$, and let $B\in\mathbb Z[x]$ be $P$-irreducible and
divisible by $P$, of degree $d$.  Then
\[
  \sum_{i<d}\log^+|b_i|\ \ge\ d\log\frac{\rho_{\min}}2,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \Lambda(B)\ \ge\ \log|b_d|+d\log\frac{\rho_{\min}}2 .
\]
Hence every nonzero $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by $P$ has
$\Lambda(F)\ge\sum_s(\deg B_s)\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ over its blocks.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every split $m\in\{1,\ldots,d\}$ of $B$ is unclean at some $\alpha_j$, with
$\rho_j\ge\rho_{\min}$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:gausscarry} there is $i<m$ with
$|b_i|\ge(\rho_{\min}/2)^{m-i}$.  For a fixed $i$, the splits charged to it
this way have distinct $m-i\ge1$ with
$(m-i)\log(\rho_{\min}/2)\le\log|b_i|$, so there are at most
$\log^+|b_i|/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ of them.  All $d$ splits are charged, which
is the first inequality.  The rest follows from $\log^+|b_d|\ge\log|b_d|$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussblocks}.
\end{proof}

Compare Proposition~\ref{prop:noangle}: $x^{2M}+\rho^{2M}$ pays
$\Lambda=2M\log\rho$ for its degree $2M$, which the theorem allows; what the
theorem forbids, at Gaussian roots, is a block of large degree and small
mass.  So a cheap multiple of a Gaussian pair product, if one exists, has
small degree, and this is what makes the mass problem finite.

\begin{corollary}[Finite reduction of the mass problem]\label{cor:gaussfinite}
Assume $\rho_{\min}>2$ and let $X>0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If every $P$-irreducible $B\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by $P$ with
$\deg B<X/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ has $\Lambda(B)\ge X$, then every nonzero
$F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by $P$ has $\Lambda(F)\ge X$.
\item The infimum of $\Lambda(F)$ over nonzero $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by
$P$ is a minimum, attained by a $P$-irreducible multiple of degree at most
$\Lambda(P)/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ whose coefficients have modulus at most
$e^{\Lambda(P)}$; in particular it is computable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
1.  Let $B$ be any block of $F$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussblocks}).  If
$\deg B<X/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ then $\Lambda(F)\ge\Lambda(B)\ge X$ by
hypothesis; otherwise Theorem~\ref{thm:gausscharge} gives
$\Lambda(F)\ge\Lambda(B)\ge\deg B\cdot\log(\rho_{\min}/2)\ge X$.

2.  $P$ itself competes, so the infimum is at most $\Lambda(P)$.  If
$\Lambda(F)\le\Lambda(P)$, any block $B$ of $F$ has $\Lambda(B)\le\Lambda(F)$,
degree at most $\Lambda(P)/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:gausscharge}, and $\log^+|b_i|\le\Lambda(P)$ for every $i$.
There are finitely many such integer polynomials.
\end{proof}

For the certificates below, rows at one degree control all smaller degrees.

\begin{lemma}[Rows are monotone in the degree]\label{lem:rowmono}
For $d\ge2K$ and $k\ge1$ let $\beta_k(d)$ be the infimum of $b_k(G)$ over
monic $G\in\R[x]$ of degree $d$ divisible by $P$.  Then
$\beta_k(d+1)\le\beta_k(d)$, and every $B\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by $P$
with $\deg B\le d$ has $b_k(B)\ge|\operatorname{lead}B|\,\beta_k(d)\ge\beta_k(d)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$xG$ has the same nonzero coefficients as $G$.  For $B$, the polynomial
$B/\operatorname{lead}B$ is a monic real multiple of $P$ of degree $\deg B$, and
$b_k$ scales by $|\operatorname{lead}B|\ge1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Certified instances]\label{prop:gaussinst}
Every nonzero $F\in\mathbb Z[x]$ divisible by the product of the displayed
Gaussian pairs satisfies the displayed bound, which exceeds the charge
$\sum_jj\log(c_{(j)}^2-1)$ that Corollary~\ref{cor:imag} makes for the
purely imaginary pairs $\pm ic_j$ with the same imaginary parts:
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\text{pairs} & \Lambda(F)\ \ge & \sum_jj\log(c_{(j)}^2-1)\\[2pt]
1\pm5i,\ -2\pm7i & 11.09 & 10.92\\
1\pm5i,\ -2\pm7i,\ 3\pm11i & 26.20 & 25.28\\
1\pm97i,\ -2\pm101i,\ 3\pm103i & 58.22 & 55.42\\
1\pm1009i,\ -2\pm1013i,\ 3\pm1019i,\ -4\pm1021i & 139.8 & 138.5
\end{array}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $X$ be the right-hand charge and $d_0$ the least integer with
$d_0\log(\rho_{\min}/2)\ge X$, that is, with
$(\rho_{\min}^2/4)^{d_0}\ge\prod_j(c_{(j)}^2-1)^{2j}$, an integer comparison:
$d_0=12,28,15,23$ for the four sets in order ($\rho_{\min}^2=26,26,9410,
1018082$).  A block of degree at least $d_0$ has
$\Lambda\ge d_0\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$, which exceeds $11.23$, $26.20$,
$58.22$ and $143.1$ respectively (Theorem~\ref{thm:gausscharge}).  A block $B$ of degree at most $d_0-1$ has,
by Lemma~\ref{lem:rowmono},
$\Lambda(B)\ge\log|\operatorname{lead}B|+\sum_{k\le K}\log^+b_k(B)
\ge\sum_{k\le K}\log^+\beta_k(d_0-1)$, the $K$ largest nonleading magnitudes
sitting at distinct positions.  For each $k$ and each set $U$ of $k-1$
excluded positions, $\min\max_{j\notin U}|f_j|$ over monic multiples of degree
$d_0-1$ is a linear program with rational data, which we solved in exact
rational arithmetic, each optimum re-certified by a weak-duality
certificate.  The minima over $U$ are
\[
  \beta_1(11)\ge1304.02,\quad\beta_2(11)\ge50.73
\]
for the first set, so $\Lambda(B)\ge\log(1304.02\cdot50.73)>11.09$, and
\[
  \beta_1(14)\ge1.0189\cdot10^{12},\quad\beta_2(14)\ge3.931\cdot10^8,\quad
  \beta_3(14)\ge9.878\cdot10^7
\]
for the third, so $\Lambda(B)>65.8$.  For the second set,
$\beta_1(27)\ge161134$, $\beta_2(27)\ge6652.3$, $\beta_3(27)\ge504.6$, so
$\Lambda(B)>27.01$; for the fourth,
$\beta_1(22)\ge1.1308\cdot10^{24}$, $\beta_2(22)\ge4.386\cdot10^{18}$,
$\beta_3(22)\ge1.082\cdot10^{18}$, so $\Lambda(B)>139.8$.  Lemma~\ref{lem:gaussblocks} gives
$\Lambda(F)\ge\Lambda(B)$ for any block $B$ of $F$.
\end{proof}

At these roots the rows exceed the imaginary rows of
Corollary~\ref{cor:imag}, some by far: $\beta_3(14)\approx9.9\cdot10^7$
against $c_{(3)}^2-1=10608$ for the second set, and for $1\pm5i$, $-2\pm7i$,
$3\pm11i$ the exact value $\beta_3(27)\ge504.6$ exceeds $c_{(3)}^2-1=120$,
and floating-point linear programs up to degree $12$ give a fourth
coefficient near $124$, which three purely imaginary pairs never force.  The bottleneck in the certificates is
the degree-charging side, not the rows.

\begin{proposition}[No exact transfer at register-type pairs]
\label{prop:gaussnotransfer}
Let $\alpha=a+ic\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with $ac\ne0$ and $|a|\ne|c|$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item No power $\alpha^m$, $m\ge1$, is real.  Hence no real-rooted
$R\in\R[y]$ and $m\ge1$ have $(x-\alpha)\mid R(x^m)$:
Theorem~\ref{thm:transfer} cannot turn such a pair into real roots.
\item If $\alpha'=a'+ic'$ has $0<|a|<c$, $0<|a'|<c'$, and $c,c'$ are powers of
distinct primes, then $\alpha,\alpha'$ do not lie on a common ray, so
Corollary~\ref{cor:ray} does not apply to the two pairs together.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
1.  If $\alpha^m$ were real, $(\alpha/\bar\alpha)^m=1$, so $\alpha/\bar\alpha$
would be a root of unity in $\mathbb Q(i)$, hence one of $\pm1,\pm i$.  The
values $\pm1$ make $\alpha$ real or purely imaginary; $\pm i$ make
$\alpha^2=\pm i|\alpha|^2$, so $a^2=c^2$.  All are excluded.  A root $\alpha$
of $R(x^m)$ has $\alpha^m$ a root of $R$.

2.  A common ray means $a/c=a'/c'$, so $ac'=a'c$; as $\gcd(c,c')=1$, $c\mid a$,
contradicting $0<|a|<c$.
\end{proof}

In Polynomial's construction the register operands satisfy $|a|\le A$, and
all but $O(\pi(A))$ register roots $a\pm p^bi$ have $p>A$.  Among those,
two roots with distinct primes are covered by item~2, and two with one prime
and exponents $b<b'$ would need $a'=ap^{b'-b}$, impossible for
$0<|a|,|a'|\le A<p$.  So neither exact reduction of this section reaches
them.

\paragraph{What remains open.}  Rows $k\ge2$, or a mass bound of order
$\sum_jj\log\rho_j$, for pairs $a_j\pm ic_j\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with $a_j\ne0$ and
general $K$.  By Corollary~\ref{cor:gaussfinite} a mass bound $X$ needs to be
proved only for $P$-irreducible multiples of degree below
$X/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$, which for $X$ of the target order
$\sum_jj\log(c_j^2-1)$ is about $K(K+1)\log c_{\max}/\log(\rho_{\min}/2)$;
beyond that degree Theorem~\ref{thm:gausscharge} pays.  In that range the
angle mechanism of Proposition~\ref{prop:noangle} is not excluded, since it
needs degree only about $\pi K/\delta$ for pairs within $\delta$ of the
imaginary axis.  Perturbing the certificate of Corollary~\ref{cor:imag} to the
nodes $1/\alpha_j$ did not close the gap either: the excluded coefficients
are unbounded, so the certificate must vanish there exactly, and the
correction needed for that is controlled only through the individual
certificate coefficients, which can be as large as an inverse Vandermonde
determinant in the close nodes $c_j^{-2}$; we obtained smallness only when
$D\max_j|a_j|/c_j$ is below that size, which integrality ($|a_j|\ge1$)
rules out.  Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussinst} settles
individual root sets; the question is a uniform statement in $K$.
